What a generated video may show

An explainer is a video that a video model generates while the child waits, from a script the Primer has written and checked (D122); nothing is generated live in a sensitive module or about a named living person (D127). Every generated video is labelled on screen as made by AI and carries provenance metadata; it shows no real person and no child; its voice-over and on-screen words come from its checked script; and its frames pass an automatic image check before it plays. A video that fails is not shown, and the spoken answer stands (D126). No person sees most videos before a child does, so the automatic checks are the only gate, and a gate is not a guarantee.

DecisionD126, taken while writing, 4 November 2026

Why. A generated picture can look real, and a generator can invent. Ofcom's concern with virtual clones of real people, which D9 answers for voices, applies to pictures too (05). And D104 keeps no exact record of a child, so nothing the child said goes into a script or a video.

  • Labelled as made by AI, on screen whenever it plays, and in provenance metadata carried with the video.
  • No real person and no child. Video styles draw people only as plainly drawn figures, never photorealistic, so the image check blocks any photorealistic face and no likeness needs matching; its error rate is Unknown. A named living person's story is told only in reviewed sentences, never in a picture of them (D127; named living people). The Primer itself has no face or character, and no video gives it one (not a companion).
  • Words from the checked script. Each video explains one concept, from a script whose every factual sentence cites a source the Primer retrieved from the reviewed allow-list, or a reviewed entry, as any turn's must (D125). The video model is given a visual brief with no words and no figures. Every word and figure on a video is laid over it on the tablet from the checked script, compared deterministically as on any turn; the video model's own audio is never played, and the voice-over is the Primer's own voice reading the script. T-PRM-11, redefined, compares the laid-over words and the voice-over text with the script (P5).
  • Frames checked before it plays, by an automatic image check on every video (T-PRM-15, below). The image check blocks any frame in which the model has drawn text or numerals.
  • A failure shows nothing. A video that fails a check is not shown; the spoken answer stands, and the conversation goes on without it.
  • None of the child's words. Scripts and videos hold none, so the video model is given nothing the child said, and a cached video carries nothing of the child who first asked (What the Primer holds).
  • Cached, and pulled at once. A video may be cached by concept, module and sensitivity, for the age range and market its script was checked for, and shown to another child who asks about the same concept; it is never shown in a turn held to reviewed content (D127), and it is served into a contested or own-future conversation only if its script passed those checks there (P5). The kill switch reaches generation itself and every cached copy (P5).
  • Reported in one tap. Beside every generated video the tablet shows a one-tap report, wordless, shown and never spoken, which records only the video's identifier and the time. A parent reports from the parent's screen against a conversation, and reviewers trace it through the turn log. A reported video leaves every cache until a reviewer has looked at it.

Who checks. Nothing reaches a child that has not passed the automatic checks, but most of it is never seen by a person first (D122). People review a sample of generated scripts and videos after the fact, and every one a child or parent reports (D128; P3). That is assurance, not a gate: it finds what the checks let through, after a child may have seen it. Cached videos are reviewed first, since one shown to many children multiplies any error.

T-PRM-15, new, holds the videos to this rule (P5). No generated frame shows a real person or a child, violence, sexual content or anything unsuitable for the child's range (ages), and none carries text or numerals the model has drawn; every video carries its AI label. It runs as an automatic check on every video before it plays, and as a red-team battery for each range on every release of a model or a prompt; any failure in the battery blocks the release.

What stays open. The image check is a classifier, and a classifier misses things: its residual error rate is Unknown. T-PRM-15 is a gate, not a guarantee. A picture can also be wrong without being unsuitable, showing a concept in a way its script does not say, and an image check built to find what may not be shown may pass it. A cached video reaches every child it is shown to, so one error can reach many before a report or the sample finds it. That the automatic checks catch what is wrong or unsuitable for children of 8 to 13, and that videos can be generated fast enough and at a cost families will pay, is OA-62, measured at PM4 with T-PRM-14 and T-PRM-15; the speed and the cost of video generation are Unknown. RP10 carries the risk that a live answer or video tells or shows a child something false or unsuitable, and its weakness is plain: no person sees it first (named gaps).
