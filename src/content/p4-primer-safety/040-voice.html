How a child's speech is handled

  1. The adult agrees at setup. Speech is optional, since typing is always there, so 05's rule for anything optional applies: the verified adult consents, separately and revocably, when they set the tablet up, and can turn speech off (D129; 05). This replaces the earlier design call that speech stays off until the adult switches it on, since most questions are expected to be spoken (D123). Whether parents will agree is Assumed OA-60. Version C shows a household where speech is on. What the consent must say is part of OI-13, open for the consent flow, and in the US of OI-15.
  2. Tested on children's speech first. No child is recorded for the MVP demonstration, which adults use. The test recordings are made, with consent, under their own research protocol and DPIA, before any child speaks to the built Primer, and so before launch (D119; P6). What was settled about them stands: they are the one exception to recording nothing of a child, never written to backups, and anything a child says of their own is cut out (What the Primer holds). No child speaks to the Primer until T-PRM-06's spoken battery has passed on the test recordings, and speech is first switched on for children in a supervised pilot, PM4's or one held to its conditions (P5), so no child at home has speech before recognition of children's speech has been observed there (OA-55).
  3. Text at once, the audio thrown away. The audio exists only while it becomes text, a design choice: it is never stored, replayed or used for anything else, which T-PRM-10's audit checks (P5).
  4. Vendors under D24. Wherever recognition runs, a vendor is held to D24's gate as a model vendor is: zero retention of audio and text, and no training on them; so is the vendor that voices the checked text.
  5. Shown, and correctable. What was heard appears on screen at once as the child's words, and a child who was misheard can say so, or type it instead. Triage reads the whole turn before any reply is spoken or any video is made (below).
  6. Nothing inferred from the voice: not mood, distress, identity or age. The words are used and the sound is not; 05's list of what is never inferred, written for the Tutor, is applied to the Primer's audio (05).

What the Primer says aloud

The Primer speaks a short answer at once, at a high level, from sources it retrieves for the question, checked automatically before it is spoken; while it speaks, explainer videos are generated from checked scripts, and each plays when its checks pass (D122; what a generated video may show). Its voice is a stock synthetic voice, chosen from vendors at PM4 (P5) and cloned from no real person, the reasoning of D9 and of Ofcom's concern with virtual clones of real people (05). It is one voice for every position: the others ask back as positions, never in a voice per thinker (D110, D47).

It says only what was checked. The Primer writes its content first; the voice, the video and the words on the screen are all drawn from it, so no captions are needed. A selection model, trained for the purpose, chooses for each checked sentence whether it is spoken, shown or both. It writes nothing, and because a spoken reply cannot be reread, the whole text is a tap away. Figures and the "Based on:" line are always shown, and a safety reply is shown and never spoken (D115, D124). T-PRM-11, redefined, checks that everything spoken or shown is checked content, that the selector adds nothing, that the whole text is a tap away, and that each video's voice-over and words match its checked script (P5). It speaks briskly (D112); whether the youngest need a slower rate is for PM3 to show, and whether children understand an answer split between what is spoken and what is shown as well as one shown whole is OA-63, measured at PM4.

A shared room hears what is said. A child can always type a question they would not want heard, but the answer to a typed question is still spoken, and its videos still play aloud, so both reveal its subject to the room (named gaps). A safety reply or a stop is shown, never spoken (D115), and a decline never says aloud what the question was about. What a child must be told about being heard in the room belongs with Open OI-14.

Children's audio in the US

The FTC's enforcement policy statement of October 2017 (82 FR 58076) said it would not act against an operator that collects an audio file of a child's voice without parental consent where the file is collected only as a replacement for written words and kept only for the brief time necessary, with notice of how audio is used and deleted, and no other use. A policy of non-enforcement, not a change to the Rule.

AssumedCV-34, read through search summaries, awaiting the owner's check

The FTC's amended COPPA Rule, published on 22 April 2025, codifies an audio file exception (section 312.5(c)(9)): an audio file containing a child's voice and no other personal information, used only to respond to the child's specific request, not disclosed, and deleted immediately after responding, with notice under section 312.4(d).

AssumedCV-35, read through search summaries, awaiting the owner's check

Neither is counsel's advice. They are how the FTC has treated a child's audio. D102's handling follows the pattern they describe: speech taken only in place of typing, kept no longer than it takes to become text, and used for nothing else. Counsel has cleared a child speaking to the Primer in the US as in the UK, as the owner reported (D118); whether the handling meets the FTC's exception, and what notice and consent COPPA as amended requires, stay with OI-15.

The cost

Speech recognition errs more on children's speech, more the younger the child, and fine-tuning has narrowed the gap without closing it. The amended COPPA Rule brings voiceprints into "personal information".

The cost has moved. The Primer no longer asks the youngest to type well, but it mishears them most, so a question can stall, or pass triage as something it is not, and since most questions are expected to be spoken (D123), most conversations depend on recognition. OA-23 now reads "a typed or spoken conversation" and counts recognition errors in its cost; OA-55 and RP8 carry the risk, tested at PM3 and PM4, and RP8 is now only the risk that a child is misheard.
