D89 to D100: Foundation in the classroom

All dated 29 October 2026, when the owner replaced Foundation's design for a parent at home with a classroom instrument for the teacher, and made the film the front page shows. D89 to D95 are the owner's; D96 to D100 were taken while rewriting Foundation's documents and are the owner's to keep or reverse. For Foundation only, they supersede D43, D50's camera half, D54's letter, D55 and D56's first half, and withdraw D42's reasons while keeping its order; each of those entries says so, and D50 still governs the Primer. None reopens D1 to D14. The brief that records them is FOUNDATION_CLASSROOM_BRIEF.md at the repository root.

The owner's decisions

D89. Foundation is a purpose-built tool that helps teachers understand how the children in their class are doing. It is sold to schools first, as a tool for the teacher, not to parents and not for home use. It runs in the background of a normal lesson, and the teacher taps nothing during it. It covers maths, verbal and non-verbal reasoning, and motor skills. It replaces the earlier design entirely.
Driven by: the owner's decision; the school is where these skills are taught, and a tool for the teacher reaches every child in a class, not only those whose parents buy it; and the owner's view that a young child is taught by a person (OA-28), which a tool for that person respects. Rejected: the earlier design, a screen coaching a parent or teaching assistant through a session with one child, which the owner replaced entirely; and a home version alongside the school one. Makes harder: the only customer is a school, with a slow sale (OA-12 sets the same expectation for the Tutor's school route) and a budget holder nobody has identified (OA-51); and hardware on every desk (D90). Supersedes D43 and D55 for Foundation. F1
D90. On every desk, a thin white flexible arm rises from a hole at the edge furthest from the chair, curves over, and ends in a small camera angled down at the desk surface. It watches the page and the hand only, never the child's face. It has no screen, no sound and no light. The desk units and the teacher's PC are hardware in the room; Foundation itself is software on the school's servers or in the cloud.
Driven by: the owner's design, as the film shows it; an arm at the far edge looks back at the hands and the page and away from the face; and the same unit on every desk singles out no child. Rejected: a camera above the class, which would see faces; a tablet or screen on the desk, which would show the child something; and D50's "no camera", for Foundation only. Makes harder: hardware on every desk, its cost unknown (OA-51); a camera in a Year 1 classroom, whose lawful basis is open (OI-28, OI-29); and two deployments to support until one is chosen. D50 still governs the Primer. F2
D91. From the camera, Foundation reads what is written, character by character, as it is written; whether an answer is right, and where in the working a mistake happened; and how the pencil is held: which fingers are on it, the grip, tripod or fist, and the pressure.
Driven by: the owner's decision; a wrong answer seen as it is written can be helped in the lesson rather than at marking; and the step where working went wrong tells the teacher what to ask. Rejected: reading only finished answers, which would miss where the working went wrong; and D56's record of what an adult tapped, for Foundation only. Makes harder: nobody knows whether an overhead camera can read a five-year-old's handwriting (OA-48), and pressure in particular may not be readable from above (OA-49); and whether a grip or handwriting read this way is biometric data is open (OI-28). F5
D92. The teacher's PC runs a desktop app on an ordinary Windows PC: a quiet class view with one tile per desk. When a child needs help, the tile turns bold and amber with the detail, and a small corner notice saying only "Foundation" appears, with a ping. The wording is plain and factual, and offers a first triage: what the problem looks like and a suggested next step. Foundation keeps a term summary for each child, which the teacher refers to on that screen at parents' evening.
Driven by: the owner's design, as the film shows it; a teacher in a lesson needs to know who, what and what to try, at a glance; and a notice that names no child keeps a child's name off a corner of the screen the class may see. Rejected: alerts on the teacher's phone, or a dashboard of scores; and anything on the child's desk. Makes harder: a teacher may find alerts during a lesson a burden, and how many are useful is unknown (OA-50); and the triage is only as good as the catalogue behind it. F2
D93. Foundation never talks to the child, never shows the child anything and never rewards the child: no stars, streaks, badges or confetti. It never points a camera or a microphone at a face. It never replaces the teacher, who is always the one who acts.
Driven by: the owner's decision; D25, which already forbids the rewards; and the owner's view that a screen in front of a young child does not help them (OA-28). Rejected: a light or a sound on the desk to tell a child they had gone wrong; and acting without the teacher, such as sending a hint to the child. Makes harder: a child gets no help from Foundation when the teacher cannot come; and a child cannot tell from the arm whether it is on, which bears on what the child must be told (OI-28). F1
D94. Foundation does not go first. That is the owner's order, and it is not a judgement that Foundation is the lesser idea. D42's three reasons described the home product and are withdrawn with it.
Driven by: the owner's decision, given without a new reason, and with the instruction that Foundation is not to be made lesser because of it. Rejected: restating D42's reasons for a product they no longer describe; and inventing new ones. Makes harder: the order cannot be argued from evidence, so evidence cannot change it; only the owner can. A reader who asks why is told it is the owner's call, and nothing more. F1
D95. The idea is named Foundation, as the film names it. The vault called it Foundations until 29 October 2026. Page addresses keep their old spelling, so that no link breaks.
Driven by: the owner's decision, and the film's name for the product. Rejected: renaming the pages, which would break every link to them, including links already shared. Makes harder: the addresses and the briefs and reports written before 29 October 2026 keep the old name, so a reader meets both. F1

Taken while rewriting Foundation's documents

D96. Recognition, not generation. The models that read the page and the hand recognise characters, marks and joints; none generates text. Every word the teacher reads is a template filled from what was read, or an entry from a reviewed catalogue of errors and next steps, chosen by rules; every answer is checked by computation, never by a model.
Driven by: D54's reason, which still holds: a product that does not converse can keep the generative model out, and with it the gate and the chance of an unsupported sentence reaching a teacher about a child; and D27's rule that a model never decides what computation can. Rejected: a model that writes each alert, which would adapt better to an unusual mistake; and a model asked whether an answer is right. Makes harder: a mistake the catalogue does not hold gets a plain description and no triage; and the catalogue is a large piece of reviewed writing (F3). Supersedes D54's letter for Foundation. F5
D97. Nothing seen is kept. Frames are processed as they arrive and discarded; no image, video or audio is stored or sent anywhere beyond the processing service. A frame in which a face appears is discarded unread. The desk unit has no microphone.
Driven by: a picture that is not kept cannot leak, be misused or be asked for later; the owner's rule that the camera never sees a face (D90, D93); and a child's voice raises the questions in OI-17, which nothing in the design needs to answer. Rejected: keeping frames to check alerts or to train models, which would make Foundation a store of video of young children; and sending frames to a third-party model vendor. Makes harder: an alert cannot be checked against the picture that raised it; the models must learn from data collected separately, with consent (F3); and nothing seen can answer a later question about what happened at a desk. F4
D98. Foundation holds, for each child, what it read in each lesson and the term summary built from it, as the school's record, kept for the school year and deleted when the child leaves the class. It never infers or names a difficulty or condition, and never ranks a child against classmates in anything a parent sees.
Driven by: D56's second half, kept: inferring a difficulty from a record would be profiling a young child on something close to health data (OI-13); and a term summary for parents' evening needs the term's readings and no more. Rejected: a record that follows the child from year to year, which a school might want; flags for possible difficulties, which schools would ask for first; and class rankings. Makes harder: Foundation cannot help identify a child who needs a specialist; a teacher next year starts without it; and whether the record is the school's is a question for counsel (OI-19). F4
D99. A child whose parent objects is not watched: that desk's camera stays off, and its tile says so. The teacher can turn any desk's camera off from the class view at any time, and does for a child who asks. The school tells the class what the arm does, in words children of this age understand.
Driven by: a camera a parent cannot refuse would not be accepted, whatever the law requires (OA-52); a child's own wish not to be watched deserves the same answer; and children should know what the arm on their desk is for. Rejected: a class where every desk must be on for Foundation to run. Taken pending OI-28; it answers nothing about it, and if counsel requires consent rather than an objection, every camera starts off instead. Makes harder: a class view with gaps in it; and a child whose desk is off gets no alerts. F4
D100. Before a lesson the teacher tells Foundation what the class is working on: a task from Foundation's library, sums from the board, or free writing. During the lesson the teacher does nothing with it.
Driven by: D89's rule that the teacher taps nothing during a lesson; and Foundation needs to know whether it is reading sums, a library task or a sentence. Rejected: asking Foundation to work out the task from the page alone, which would make its first reading a guess. Makes harder: one more step before every lesson; and a lesson that changes halfway is read as the task it was set as. F2
