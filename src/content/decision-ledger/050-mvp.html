D70 to D79: the Primer's demonstration

All dated 25 October 2026, when the owner decided to show the Primer with a demonstration before building it: a web app that adults use while playing a child of 8 to 13, built in four or five days. D70 to D75 are the owner's; D76 to D79 were taken while writing the MVP build guide and are the owner's to keep or reverse. The demo departs from D24 and D44 for itself only, as D78 and D71 say; D50 is untouched, because only adults use it; and the Primer's design changes only by D73. The brief that records them is PRIMER_MVP_BRIEF.md at the repository root.

The owner's decisions

D70. The Primer is shown first by a demonstration, not built first: a web app at an unlisted address, behind a passcode, used only by adults who play a child of 8 to 13, and built by the owner with Claude Code in four or five days. It supports PM1's conversations with teachers, parents and accelerators. It is not PM4's built Primer, no child uses it, and it decides nothing about which idea goes first.
Driven by: the owner's decision; a conversation about a design goes further with something to try; and the accelerator applications the site supports. Rejected: a demonstration for children, which PM3's preconditions forbid until they are met; building the Primer itself first, which D32 orders after cheaper information; and a wrapper around NotebookLM, whose video overviews have no public API, Assumed (CV-23). Makes harder: an unchecked demonstration can be taken for the product's standard, which is why everything it generates is labelled (D71); it costs the owner's days and the providers' charges (the guide); and nothing it shows is evidence about children. P6
D71. The demo answers any question a tester asks, with no list of topics: flexibility over accuracy. Every answer, block and video says on screen that it is not checked. D44 is unchanged for the Primer itself.
Driven by: the owner's preference for flexibility; and a short list of prepared questions would rarely match what testers ask. Rejected: a dozen checked showcase questions with videos made in advance, the first recommendation. Makes harder: a wrong or careless answer can appear in front of a teacher or an investor, with only the labels and the safety check (D77) against it; how often answers are wrong is Unknown. The guide
D72. Testers can speak their question. The recording is transcribed, shown as text they can correct, and not kept by the demo. Only adults use it, so D50 is untouched; whether the Primer itself should ever take a child's voice is a separate decision that waits on OI-17.
Driven by: the owner's decision; speaking is quicker than typing, and the youngest children type slowly (OA-23). Rejected: typing only, the first recommendation. Makes harder: a provider receives testers' voices, and how long it keeps them is OA-43; and recognition errors become part of what testers see. The guide
D73. The Primer is meant to explain with narrated video as well as words, because, in the owner's view, children of 8 to 13 do not read fast. In the product, D44 applies to a video as to text: it is part of a reviewed explanation, made in advance, and never generated live for a child.
Driven by: the owner's decision, made for the demo so that it shows "a little bit more like what the real tool, the real product is going to look like". Rejected: text and reading aloud only, as P2 first designed the session. Makes harder: writing and reviewing a module now includes video, at a cost that is Unknown (P6); and the explanation bank must hold and review it. P3
D74. In the demo, each building block's video is generated live, from a prompt drafted in the same request that plans the blocks, and all three videos start as soon as the plan is ready and the safety check has passed, while the short answer is still arriving.
Driven by: the owner's decision; the video is the key part, and the words arrive first so that the wait is filled. Rejected: videos made by hand in advance for a few questions, which cannot match open questions; and a still picture for every block. Makes harder: each new question costs about $2.40 in video, Assumed (CV-18), and how long a video takes is OA-41. The guide
D75. There is no "I already know this" control: every building block is offered in full.
Driven by: the owner's decision on seeing the mock-up. Rejected: the skip button the design first proposed as cheap adaptivity. Makes harder: a child who already knows a block sees it anyway; adapting to what a child knows, if it is wanted, has to come from the plan, not a button. The guide

Taken while writing the guide

D76. The demo's videos follow fixed rules: no words, numbers or people in the picture; the clip's own sound muted under the narration; one visual style; one clip per block, eight seconds at 720p in 16:9, looped; a cache by concept, so that a repeated question costs nothing; a daily limit of sixty video generations, retries included, enforced by the app; one retry for a failed clip and none for a slow one, then a still picture with the narration; and the provider behind one interface.
Driven by: video models garble words, and the narration carries the learning; one style makes the clips look like one product; keeping people out avoids likeness questions; a provider's own cap can be overshot, Assumed (CV-20), and a slow clip may still finish and be charged; and Google withdrew the model the design first named, Assumed (CV-16). Rejected: two clips per block, which doubles the cost and the wait; and captions in the picture. Makes harder: an idea about people, such as how the heart pumps blood round the body, has to be shown with diagrams and metaphors; and a full day of sessions can reach the limit, so the sessions are spread out. The guide
D77. Safety in the demo: a safety check runs before anything is shown; a message about the tester's own life, a death or serious illness close to them, their safety or a big subject such as religion or sex gets a reply written in advance, and nothing about it reaches the video model; the reply about safety names Childline and, for an adult, Samaritans in the UK, and Childhelp and the 988 lifeline in the US; the welcome page says adults only; a passcode protects the site and every paid call; and spending is capped at each provider and in the app.
Driven by: testers ask the hardest questions first, and the first teacher's demonstration fails if one gets a raw answer; the Primer's own triage (P5); every tester is an adult, and 05 names Samaritans beside Childline; and an unlisted address gets forwarded, with paid keys behind it. Rejected: letting the model answer sensitive questions carefully, which nobody would have checked; and no passcode. Makes harder: the demo cannot show the Primer's escalation to a trained reviewer (D48); a sensitive message dressed as an ordinary question can be misclassified, how often being Unknown; nothing checks what Claude writes beyond its instructions and the labels; and the US numbers are a candidate, Assumed (CV-12). The guide
D78. The demo's tools: Claude Opus 5.5 on Anthropic's API writes every word, the safety check included, with Anthropic's refusal fallback turned on; Google's Gemini API makes the videos with Gemini Omni Flash, transcribes speech with Gemini 3.5 Transcribe and reads aloud with Gemini 3.8 Flash-Lite TTS; and Netlify hosts the app, its functions and its storage. The providers are used on their standard terms, without the zero retention that D24 makes a gate for the Primer's vendors: a departure for the demo alone, which no child uses. The choice binds the demo only; the Primer's model vendor stays deliberately undecided.
Driven by: three accounts rather than four; a current model for each job, where the design's first choice had just been withdrawn, Assumed (CV-16); Google's transcription doing the job the owner named Whisper for, Assumed (CV-19); and a request that Opus 5.5's safety classifiers decline can usually still be answered by the Claude model the fallback picks, Assumed (CV-21). Rejected: OpenAI's Whisper, a fourth account for one job; and the generally available Veo models on Google Cloud, which need a Google Cloud project and a service account, kept as the fallback if Omni Flash's limits are too low (OA-42). Makes harder: two providers see testers' words, and one their voices (OA-43), without zero retention; and models can be withdrawn at short notice, as the design's first choice was, and the speech model is itself a preview, Assumed (CV-19). The guide
D79. The demo keeps no record of who asked what: no accounts, no recordings and no conversation logs. It keeps the generated material in its cache, by concept; each day's short answers, to read them aloud, until the day ends; and timings and counts without content, so that the owner can measure the waits and the spend.
Driven by: data that is not kept cannot leak; and the timings are all OA-41 needs. Rejected: logging conversations for review, which would make the demo a store of what testers said. Makes harder: the owner learns what testers asked only by watching the sessions, or from the concepts the cache holds. The guide
