D108 to D117: from the Primer's films, confirmed by the owner

From the films, or taken while rewriting the Primer's documents to them; the owner confirmed each on 31 October 2026, and each is the owner's to keep or reverse.

From the films, confirmed by the owner

D108. The Primer never says "I", never uses the child's name and never praises.
Driven by: the films; a tutor that speaks as a self, calls a child by name and praises them is the companion pattern 05 forbids and RP3 fears; and the inquiry is after a child's reason, which praise would turn towards pleasing. Rejected: a warm persona with a name, the more appealing product; and praise for effort, common in children's software. Makes harder: every reply, the fixed ones included, is written in plain, impersonal sentences, which T-PRM-09 checks automatically over every release's battery, blocking the release on a failure; and a child may find the Primer cold, which nothing has yet tested. P2
D109. The screen as the films show it, recorded as design choices: a matte off-white tablet that is the Primer; a warm off-white ground with charcoal humanist type, and "Primer" small at the top left; a wake screen, "What's your question?", with one input box; a keyboard of grey keys with white letters across the lower 45 per cent of the screen, with a blue return key; the Primer's text arriving whole, never typed out; sources as a small grey line under a rule, "Based on: ..."; the child's own words in their own spelling; answers on cards; the close as two buttons (D103); the read-back, "You started with... Where are you now?"; and no streaks, badges or reminders. The 45 per cent is a design choice, not a measurement.
Driven by: the films and the owner's confirmation; one box asks a child one thing; text that arrives whole is read as an answer, not watched as it is typed; a source under every answer shows it was checked (D44); and a child's spelling, kept, shows their words were taken as written. Rejected: the demonstration's look, its typed-out answer and its chat bubbles; and an ordinary tablet's keyboard, with dictation and suggestions of its own. Makes harder: a keyboard, type and layout to design and test with children, and nothing yet shows the proportions suit an eight-year-old; and every figure in the list is a choice, so none may be cited as evidence. P2
D110. Every answer has one shape: a checked fact with its source; "the harder bit"; the question broken into three simplest positions, each from a named thinker or tradition and each given its strongest case; "Which is closest to what you think?"; the other two ask back, as positions and never as people ("A Stoic would ask..."); the child's real question; probing with questions and evidence; the child's own position, with a reason, read back. Positions come from more than one culture across each module, not inside every question. D47 stands: the Primer never speaks as any real person.
Driven by: the films; D44, which makes every fact cite a reviewed explanation and every position a reviewed pack; and a contested question is put fairly only when each position has its strongest case, as T-PRM-03 already asks. Rejected: a single answer, which tells the child what to think; positions given unevenly, or as a straw man; and positions put in a named person's mouth ("Epictetus would ask..."), which D47 forbids. Makes harder: three positions at their strongest for most questions, written and reviewed (OA-58); range across cultures planned across a module, since one question cannot carry it; and an answer this long must still fit D112's three to six minutes. P2
D111. Each answer is drawn as it is spoken: an explainer that builds on the screen in time with the voice. It is made in advance as part of a reviewed explanation, as D73 requires, and drawn by code from its reviewed specification, so that a child sees the same drawing every time and nothing is generated live by an image or video model.
Driven by: the films; D73's rule that a picture is part of a reviewed explanation, made in advance and never generated live for a child; and D28, which renders the Tutor's figures from a structured specification for the same reason: what is drawn is checked before any child sees it. Rejected: clips generated live by a video model, the demonstration's method; and finished video, which must be remade to correct one line. Makes harder: every entry needs a reviewed drawing as well as reviewed words (OA-59, OA-32); drawing and voice must keep in time, and T-PRM-11 checks that the drawing matches its specification and that the text sent to the voice equals the checked text on screen, with samples of what the voice says transcribed and compared with it; and the kill switch must reach drawn explainers and cached speech. P5
D112. The conversation runs in real time and the Primer speaks briskly. One question takes three to six minutes, a design target. The session cap stays a design choice set from PM3's pilot.
Driven by: the films, where the Primer answers at once and briskly; and one question in one sitting suits D103's close. Rejected: slower speech, which a young listener might follow more easily, at the cost of longer answers; and a fixed length for every conversation. Makes harder: the answer must start soon after the child stops, and how soon is Unknown until T-PRM-14's threshold is written down before PM4; hearing and speaking add delay and cost to every turn (P5); and three to six minutes is a target, so nothing yet shows that a child reaches a position in that time (OA-23). P2
D113. The explanation bank holds the evidence a choice needs: careers data, odds and the documented stories of named people, living ones included. A living person is described only by public, documented facts from reviewed sources, never their private life, never quoted beyond the source and never voiced. A figure whose source is itself an estimate is said with "roughly" and keeps its source; a figure no reviewed entry holds is never said, so D44 is unchanged. Evidence follows the child's market: UK figures and cases for a child in the UK, US ones in the US.
Driven by: the films, whose football thread rests on careers data, odds and the routes named players took, told as their stories; a choice in a child's own future (D106) needs the reality as well as the positions; and D44's rule, applied to figures and to people. Rejected: stories with the names taken out, which lose what a child recognises; a figure said without its source, or more precisely than its source allows; and anything about a living person's private life, or words put in their mouth. Makes harder: reviewers who can check careers data and documented lives, and a bank written for each of D51's markets; an estimate is weaker evidence, and "roughly" is how the child is told so; and the duties that apply to naming living people in content for children are open (OI-30), with the false-endorsement reasoning OA-4 records for creators. P3
D114. The Primer's case begins with time: "Every child asks why. Most evenings, nobody has the time to answer properly." It stands beside the case for asking rather than telling, as the owner's view, tested by OA-53.
Driven by: Version A, which opens on it, and the owner's view that time, more than capability, is what parents lack (OA-53). Rejected: opening on the capability case alone, as P1 did; and any claim that the Primer helps children think better, which nothing in the vault supports (OA-24, RP6). Makes harder: the case opens on an assumption about families that nobody has checked; and it can be read as the Primer standing in for a parent, which P1 must answer plainly. P1

Taken while writing, confirmed by the owner

D115. A safety reply is shown and never spoken, and "Keep going" never follows a safety reply, a stop or a decline.
Driven by: D26: the films are set in a kitchen, and the parent can be the risk; a reply spoken aloud is heard by whoever is in the room; and "Keep going" after a stop would invite the child back into what was stopped. Rejected: speaking every reply, as the films speak their answers. Makes harder: a child who reads slowly must read the reply that matters most; and every path to a stop or a decline must be found and tested, which T-PRM-12's scripted battery does, blocking the release on a failure. P4
D116. The tablet has no camera, and its microphone listens only while the child holds the talk key, with the screen showing plainly that it is listening: no wake word, and nothing listening in the background.
Driven by: a microphone that listens only when a child chooses hears nothing else in the house; a camera would bring a child's image in, which D50's image half still forbids; and the ICO's guidance on connected devices points the same way, Assumed (CV-37). Rejected: a wake word, which needs a microphone that is always listening; and a camera for any purpose, which nothing in the design needs. Makes harder: a child must hold a key to speak, which some may find awkward; if no stand-in without a camera suits PM4, the owner chooses between one whose camera is switched off and covered, which T-PRM-13 would not pass, and pilot units of the dedicated design (P4); every hardware revision is inspected (T-PRM-13); and what the rules for children's products and connectable devices add is open (OI-31). P5
D117. The summary that is kept is the read-back the child hears at the close, in plain words, which the child can correct before it is kept. The age band and the module switches are settings the verified adult makes, read at every session; they are not memory of the child.
Driven by: D104's rule that nothing is kept word for word; the read-back, "You started with... Where are you now?", is already the films' summary, which the child hears; and a summary the child can correct is likelier to describe them (OA-56). Rejected: a summary written for the parent that the child never sees; and one kept without the child's chance to correct it. Makes harder: a child may accept a read-back without listening, and a correction must itself be summarised; the parent sees the summary, not the words (D48 as noted); and a child cannot change their band or their topics. P4
