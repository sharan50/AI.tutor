Primer: content and evidence

Everything the Primer says comes from content a person wrote and a different person reviewed: an explanation bank for the factual part of a child's question, and inquiry packs that set out what named thinkers have argued, for the part people disagree about. That catalogue is the Primer's asset and its bottleneck. The evidence that this kind of teaching improves how children reason is weaker than the case for exam tutoring, and this document says how weak and how it would be measured.

Where every statement comes from

Every factual statement the Primer makes cites a reviewed explanation, and every philosophical position it presents cites a reviewed inquiry pack that names the thinker or tradition. A question neither can answer gets an honest "not yet" and becomes a request for new content.

DecisionD44, 22 October 2026

This is the Tutor's founding rule, that nothing reaches a learner which cannot be traced to a checked source (03), carried into a domain where the risk is influence rather than a wrong mark. A Primer allowed to answer from the model's general knowledge would be a free chatbot with a child's interface, and nothing about it could be warranted to a parent.

The cost is stated in D44 itself: at launch the Primer will say "not yet" often, and how often is Unknown until children use it (OA-32). That cost is accepted because the alternative is a product whose most important property, that it can be trusted with a child's questions, rests on nothing.

The explanation bank

The explanation bank answers the factual part of the questions children actually ask: about nature and the body, the sky and the sea, machines, history, words and numbers. It is not a syllabus and it is not exam content; that is the Tutor's item bank.

Each entry holds:

  • The questions it answers, in the several ways children phrase them.
  • An explanation written for an age band, in short plain sentences. Where one explanation cannot serve the whole of 8 to 13, there are versions for the code's age ranges within it (P4).
  • What is not known, or where scientists disagree, said as plainly as what is known. A child should learn early that "we don't know yet" is an answer adults give.
  • Bridges to the deeper questions an explanation raises, each pointing at an inquiry pack.
  • A narrated video, where the entry has one: a short visual explainer with spoken words written for it, made in advance and reviewed with the text (D73). What one costs to make and review is Unknown.
  • Provenance: the writer, the reviewer, the reference works consulted, the date and the version.

Entries carry identifiers of the form EB-SCI-SLEEP-002, and the Primer shows the identifier beside every factual turn, as the Tutor's transcripts show the item-bank node behind each statement (01), so a parent or reviewer can look the source up.

When a child asks something the bank cannot answer, the question is separated from the child's account and rewritten in general terms, with names, places and anything about the child's own life removed, then counted and added to a writing queue ordered by how often children ask it. The queue never holds what a child typed, or any link back to the child; who or what does the rewriting, and how it is checked, is set with the safeguarding policy (P4). The queue is the content roadmap: the catalogue grows towards what children want to know, not towards what writers find interesting. The rate at which it can grow is set by reviewers, not writers, and is Unknown until PM2 times it.

Below the grounding floor, the affected module stops composing factual answers and serves the entry text verbatim or "not yet" until the cause is found. The floor is the Tutor's P2 floor, 99.5% of sampled turns, adopted as a design choice rather than derived. D21 carries over with it: grounding is reported per session, not per turn, and no claim is made that the Primer "does not make things up".

The inquiry packs

An inquiry pack is built around one question that people have argued about, such as "can you know you are not dreaming?" or "if you replace every part of a bicycle, is it the same bicycle?". It holds:

  • The question, phrased for each age band.
  • Positions, each with the thinker or tradition named, a plain paraphrase, the strongest reason given for it, and where the writer found it.
  • Counterexamples and thought experiments that test each position, cutting in more than one direction.
  • Starter moves for each band: the first questions to ask a child, and the counterexample to try first.
  • A contested flag. Most pack questions are contested. The flag switches on the check that the Primer never states a conclusion as its own (T-PRM-03).

Positions are attributed, never asserted. The Primer reports that Descartes argued something; it never adds that he was right. On a contested question every pack carries at least two positions, each at its strongest, and the balance reviewer checks that none is framed more favourably than the others.

The packs draw on several traditions, deliberately. A Primer that drew only on European philosophy would be teaching a child, without saying so, whose thinking counts. The range intended looks like this:

Chinese thought, such as Zhuangzi's butterfly dream, or Mencius and Xunzi disagreeing about whether people are good by nature; Indian philosophy, such as the Nyaya school's account of the ways we come to know things; Islamic philosophy, such as Ibn Sina's "floating man" thought experiment about the self; the Southern African idea of ubuntu, that a person is a person through other people; ancient Greek and modern European philosophy; and philosophy written for children today. These are examples to show the range intended, not content: each attribution is a writer's to make and a reviewer's to check.

Modules, and which are sensitive

The Primer's subjects come in modules the company writes and reviews. Parents switch modules on and off; modules on sensitive subjects start switched off; each module is written for an age band and offered only within it. Parents cannot add content or views.

DecisionD46, 22 October 2026

A module groups related packs and explanation-bank entries under a theme, with a description and example questions a parent reads before deciding. A subject is sensitive where families' beliefs differ, or where a child's own life is likely to come up: morality, death, religion and belief, politics and power. Sensitive modules start switched off; switching one on does not loosen anything else, so the contested-question check and triage apply inside it exactly as outside.

A first sketch to test the idea, not a catalogue. The modules, their bands and the sensitive flag are set by the review group at PM2. Sensitive modules are written for 10 and over, so a child of 8 or 9 is offered none (D57). Every band is made of whole steps of the Primer's three, 8 and 9, 10 to 12 and 13, and each step sits inside one of the code's age ranges.

"Minds and machines" needs one rule of its own. The Primer can discuss whether a machine could think or feel, and it never uses itself as the example: it does not claim feelings and does not invite a child to wonder whether it has them, because that is the companion pattern 05 rules out (05).

Who writes, and who reviews

The writer and the reviewer are never the same person, the rule the Tutor applies to its item bank (03). Writers are philosophy educators, including people trained in Philosophy for Children, primary and lower-secondary teachers, and subject specialists for the explanation bank.

The standing review group is the one page 13 requires: child-safety and education expertise, with the power to block a release, inspect red-team results and require a rollback (13). Because the owner wants the UK and the US at once (D51), review includes whether a module reads acceptably to families across the range of beliefs in both countries, and whether one catalogue can serve both is an assumption, OA-27.

Provenance is recorded per entry and every change is reviewed again. This is a rate-limited human process that does not get cheaper when the next model ships, which makes it the Primer's equivalent of the Tutor's examiner supply (12, R7). Unknown the cost of a module and the number of modules a review group can clear; PM2 measures both, and RP5 is the risk that they are worse than the plan needs.

What may not enter

  • Reproduced copyrighted text. Philosophers are paraphrased with attribution. A modern translation is quoted only where the review group has confirmed it may be.
  • Commercial programme materials. Published Philosophy for Children session plans and curricula do not enter. A writer trained in those methods writes original material, and declares per pack that no programme material came with them, as the Tutor's validators do (D20).
  • Anything a parent writes. Parents send ideas and requests to review; nothing they write is content (D46).
  • A child's words. A question from the writing queue is rewritten, never copied, and the catalogue never contains anything a child typed.
  • Anything without two names on it. No entry, pack or module goes live without a named writer and a different named reviewer.

What the evidence says about the method

The honest summary is that the nearest human method was evaluated in the EEF's two trials, a smaller one with small positive effects and a larger one that found no evidence of an effect on attainment Assumed (CV-05), and nothing in this vault shows that an AI-led version of it improves how children reason.

The Education Endowment Foundation evaluated Philosophy for Children twice. An efficacy trial reported in 2015, in Years 4 and 5, found small positive effects on reading and maths, at moderate security. A later and larger effectiveness trial, in Year 6, found no evidence of an effect on reading or maths, at the foundation's highest security rating, and the programme was removed from its list of promising projects. Teachers in the larger trial reported gains in pupils' respect for others' views that the pupil survey did not detect.

AssumedCV-05, awaiting the owner's check

Three consequences, all of them constraints on how the Primer is described.

  1. No attainment claim. The Primer is not sold as raising reading or maths scores. The more secure of the EEF's two trials found no evidence of such an effect for the human version Assumed (CV-05), and nothing suggests an AI-led version would do better.
  2. The outcome is reasoning itself, measured directly. If the Primer does anything, it should show in how a child gives reasons, weighs another view and responds to a counterexample. That is what T-PRM-07 measures, and the teachers' reports in the larger trial Assumed (CV-05) suggest it is at least the right thing to look for.
  3. Neither result may transfer. An AI-led dialogue with one child differs from a teacher-led discussion with a class in almost every respect that might matter: no classmates to disagree with, no teacher reading the room, and a conversation that can follow one child's question all the way down. The positive result may not carry over, and neither may the null one (OA-25).

The evidence for AI tutoring that the Tutor relies on (00) is evidence about AI supporting the teaching of a subject. It says nothing about a child's reasoning, and the Primer does not borrow it.

How we would know it works

The Primer works if children who use it reason better, unaided, than comparable children who do not. Minutes, sessions and return visits are not outcomes; page 13 rules them out as success measures (13), and D25 rules out optimising for them.

T-PRM-07 is designed like the Tutor's unaided check (D15): the measurement happens without the Primer. Children answer a short set of open questions in writing, before and after a block of sessions, and raters who do not know which group a child is in, or whether an answer came before or after, score them against a rubric:

  • gives a reason for a view;
  • considers another view, and states it fairly;
  • responds to a counterexample rather than ignoring it;
  • separates what they know from what they believe;
  • changes their mind for a reason, and says why.

That a change in how a child reasons can be measured this way, sensitively enough to show a difference in a pilot. Unknown the effect size worth detecting, the number of children that would need, and how well raters agree; all three are set with the independent evaluator at PM4.
