What this is for

Before anything is built, the owner will put each idea to the people who would have to believe in it: teachers who have taught these ages, professors who study how children learn and think, and parents who would pay. The questions are written to find out what would make each idea wrong, not to collect encouragement, and every answer is recorded against the assumption it tests.

Three decisions wait on these conversations: which idea goes first between the Tutor and the Primer (the decision rule); whether D42, that Foundations does not go first, survives contact with people who teach four-year-olds; and whether the three ideas belong together as one product (one product or three).

The conversations also settle some assumptions outright. Of the vault's assumptions, thirteen are resolvable by reading or by asking rather than by building (14). The ones that need asking are here: OA-10 and OA-17 for the Tutor, with an informed prior on OA-21; OA-22 and OA-26 for the Primer; OA-28, OA-29, OA-33 and OA-34 for Foundations. Others, such as OA-23 and OA-30, cannot be settled by asking but can be given an informed prior, which is worth having before a pilot is designed around them.

This is the first milestone of each idea: M1 for the Tutor, PM1 for the Primer (P6) and FM1 for Foundations (F6). None of it requires an engineer.

How to ask

Eight rules. The first two matter most.

  1. Ask about what people have done, not what they think of the idea. "Tell me about the last time a child asked you a question you could not follow up" produces evidence. "Would you use an AI that answers children's questions?" produces politeness.
  2. Describe the idea last. Ask about the problem first, so the answers are not shaped by the pitch. When you do describe it, ask what would make them say no, and who they think would hate it.
  3. Say that nothing is built. It is true, and it changes how freely people criticise.
  4. Count commitments, not compliments. An introduction, an offer to pilot, a deposit or a request to be told when it exists is evidence. "That sounds amazing" is not.
  5. Ask everyone the core questions in the same words, so that answers can be compared across people and across ideas.
  6. Talk to adults only. This guide collects no information about any child: no names, no ages beyond the year group, no stories that identify a child. Any later session with children, such as the human-run Primer pilot at PM3 or the Foundations pilot at FM3, needs its own written protocol, informed parental consent and safeguarding arrangements, and is out of scope here.
  7. Record against the ids. Every answer is noted with the date, the person's role, the assumption or question it bears on (OA-22, D42 and so on), what was said, and whether it supports the vault's position or cuts against it.
  8. Write down what surprised you on the same day. Surprises are the answers that most often change a plan, and the first to be forgotten.

What to do with the answers

  1. Keep one log. A row per answer: date, role, the id it bears on, what was said, and whether it supports or cuts against the vault's position. The log is the evidence; a summary written later from memory is not.
  2. Re-read the tests every few conversations. Hold the answers against which idea goes first as they arrive, rather than at the end, when the most recent conversation will weigh most.
  3. Write answers into the register. When an assumption is answered, record the answer and the date beside it in 14. The marker changes only when the owner changes it.
  4. Record the decision. When the field research decides which idea goes first, or reverses D42, the decision goes into the ledger with what was rejected and what it makes harder, as every decision in this vault does.
  5. Say what went against you. An answer that cuts against the owner's view is the most valuable kind, and the one an investor will ask about. It belongs in the document it affects, in plain words.
