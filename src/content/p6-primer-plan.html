Who pays, and why that is unknown

The payer is a parent and the user is a child, and nobody yet knows why that parent would pay. The Tutor's parent is choosing between the product and an hour of a human tutor before an examination that matters to the family (00). The Primer's parent has no examination, no deadline, and no purchase the Primer obviously replaces.

That parents of 8 to 13 year olds will pay for the Primer with no exam to prepare for. What purchase, if any, it replaces is unknown: enrichment such as clubs, classes and books; a tutor for a child who is ahead rather than behind; or nothing at all. This is the Primer's most load-bearing assumption.

AssumedOA-22, resolvable by asking parents and a landing page

Two further facts about the buyer shape the plan. The Primer cannot use engagement to keep paying families: it has no engagement objective, no streaks and no return notifications (D25), so whatever retention it has must come from a child wanting to bring the next question. And the owner's view is that the US market has proven more receptive than the UK to consumer education products for children (OA-31), which is the basis for launching in both at once and which is checkable by reading published market data.

Routes: parents and schools, the UK and the US

Parents first, schools later. The first route is direct to parents, on the pattern of the Tutor's Route A (07): a verified adult holds the account and switches the modules. A route through schools, on the pattern of Route B (08), has one real advantage, which is that escalation goes to the school's designated safeguarding lead (05). It also brings the school's own frame of assessment (OA-20 applies) and, for a product that discusses politics and belief, the expectations on schools to present political issues in a balanced way (Open OI-16). It is not a launch route.

The Primer and Foundations launch in the UK and the US together where resources allow, and in the UK first where they do not. D1 continues to govern the Tutor. India is not pursued for any idea sold direct to families while section 9(3) of the DPDP Act stands.

DecisionD51, 22 October 2026

The Primer is the idea that travels best, because it is not tied to a curriculum or an examination board. A US launch still adds four things: the amended COPPA Rule and the state companion-chatbot laws (Open OI-15); a US escalation route (P4); content reviewed for families in two countries (OA-27); and the question of which entity contracts with a US parent, which extends the Tutor's Open OI-7. India is held back because the register's finding on section 9(3) prohibits behavioural monitoring of children whatever a parent consents to (04). Distribution channels for either country are Unknown, and app stores and payment providers are the chokepoint here as they are for the Tutor (04).

What would make the Primer go first

Foundations does not go first (D42); between the Tutor and the Primer, the choice is open and the evidence decides it. The tests are set out in which idea goes first. From the Primer's side, the decision rule is this.

The Primer goes ahead of the Tutor if PM1 shows all four of the following: parents of 8 to 13 year olds say, and show on a landing page, that they would pay without an exam to prepare for (OA-22); parents accept the module design rather than rejecting the idea of an AI discussing big questions with their child (OA-26); teachers and professors give no strong reason to doubt that children in the band can sustain a typed inquiry (OA-23); and counsel's answers to OI-13 and OI-15 leave the under-13 consent flow affordable. If any fails, or if the Tutor's own first milestone comes back stronger (11), the Tutor goes first and the Primer's evidence is kept for later.

Thresholds unknownwritten down before PM1 starts, as D37 did for the Tutor

The thresholds that turn those four conditions into a yes or a no are not set here. They are written down before PM1 runs, as D37 fixed the Tutor's route conditions in advance, so that the result cannot be argued with afterwards Decision D58.

Three things do not count as evidence: enthusiasm for the idea, interest from an accelerator, and how long a child stays engaged in a demonstration. Y Combinator's request names a primer (CV-01), but its ages and its order differ from this one (D42), and an application is not demand. Page 13 states the principle that applies: no stage advances because the team likes the concept or because engagement is high (13).
