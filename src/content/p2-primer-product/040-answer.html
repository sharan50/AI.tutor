What an answer looks like

Every answer has one shape. The Primer speaks a short answer at once while explainer videos are generated live, every fact cites a source it retrieved or a reviewed entry, and the sources show on a small grey line beneath.

Every answer has one shape: a checked fact with its source; "the harder bit"; the question broken into three simplest positions, each from a named thinker or tradition and each given its strongest case; "Which is closest to what you think?"; the other two ask back, as positions and never as people ("A Stoic would ask..."); the child's real question; probing with questions and evidence; the child's own position, with a reason, read back. Positions come from more than one culture across each module, not inside every question. D47 stands: the Primer never speaks as any real person.

DecisionD110, from the films, confirmed by the owner, 31 October 2026

Three positions, each at its strongest. Each names the thinker or tradition and gives the case as its own holders would make it. A reviewed inquiry pack is used first wherever one exists; otherwise the positions are generated from the sources the Primer retrieves for the turn. An attribution its source does not support is cut, and one found in a release's sample blocks the release (T-PRM-02, D125). In a sensitive module, positions come only from reviewed packs (D127). The two the child did not choose ask back in the third person, never in a thinker's own voice; where a film has a thinker ask in his own name, this page rewrites the turn (below). Range is a module's job, not a question's. Whether three such positions can be generated and attributed correctly for most questions, and written and reviewed for the sensitive modules, is OA-58 (P3).

An answer at once, and videos while it speaks. The Primer speaks a short answer at once, at a high level, from sources it retrieves for the question, checked automatically before it is spoken. While it speaks, it writes the script for each explainer, each on one specific concept, checks the script, and has a video model generate the video from it; each video plays when its checks pass (D122; P5). The conversation then goes on through the rest of the shape, to the child's own view, read back (D110). Version C shows its explainers building in time with the voice: dots thinning from the many to the few, three columns for three positions, a staircase, a two-by-two grid of footballers, a stack for a hedge. In the product each is a generated video, so that any question a child asks can have one, except in a sensitive module or about a named living person, where nothing is generated live (D127). Explainers made in advance would cover only what was written beforehand. A turn that D127 holds to reviewed content shows no generated video, cached or new, and a decline, a triage stop and a safety reply carry no video.

What a video may show. Every generated video is labelled on screen as made by AI and carries provenance metadata. It shows no real person and no child; its voice-over and on-screen words come from its checked script; its frames pass an automatic image check before it plays; and a video that fails is not shown, and the spoken answer stands (D126; P4). Scripts and videos hold none of the child's words. A video may be cached by concept and reused, and the kill switch reaches every cached copy.

Who checks. Nothing reaches a child that has not passed the automatic checks, but most of it is never seen by a person first (D122). People review a sample of generated scripts and videos after the fact, and every one a child or parent reports (D128; P3). This is the weak point, and it is plain: a live answer or video can tell or show a child something false or unsuitable, and no person sees it first (RP10). Whether videos can be generated fast enough for a conversation in real time, at a cost families will pay, with checks that catch what is wrong or unsuitable for children of 8 to 13, is OA-62, measured at PM4. The cost and speed of video generation are Unknown.

Grounded in sources. Every factual sentence cites a source the Primer retrieved for the turn from a reviewed allow-list of reference sources, or a reviewed entry where one exists, and is entailed by it; a reviewed entry is used first wherever it exists. A sentence no source supports is cut, and a turn left with nothing to say gets "not yet" (D125; P3). The bank holds the evidence a choice needs: careers data, odds and the documented stories of named people, living ones included. Facts and stories about named living people, and every sensitive module, come only from reviewed entries and packs, never from live generation (D127). A living person is described only by public, documented facts from reviewed sources, never their private life, never quoted beyond the source and never voiced. A figure whose source is itself an estimate is said with "roughly" and keeps its source. Evidence follows the child's market: UK figures and cases for a child in the UK, US ones in the US (D113; P3). What the law asks of telling children the stories of named living people is Open OI-30 (P4).

The source line. Under each answer's checked fact, a small grey line under a rule: "Based on: ...", built from the sources the Primer retrieved for the turn, or from the reviewed entry the fact comes from (D109, D125). It is always shown (D124). An entry's identifier is for reviewers, and the child never sees it.

Spoken, shown, or both. The Primer writes its content first, and the voice, the videos and the words on the screen all come from it, so no captions are needed. A selection model chooses which checked sentences are spoken and which are shown; figures and the source line are always shown, and the whole text is a tap away (D124, below).
