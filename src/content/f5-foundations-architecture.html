Foundation: architecture

Foundation is a reader and a set of rules. Models recognise what is on the page and how the hand holds the pencil; none generates a word. Every answer is checked by computation, every diagnosis of a wrong answer is computed, and every sentence the teacher reads is a template or a reviewed entry chosen by rules. Frames are read and thrown away. The hard engineering is not language but vision: reading a five-year-old's page from above, and nobody yet knows how well that can be done.

Recognition, not generation. The models that read the page and the hand recognise characters, marks and joints; none generates text. Every word the teacher reads is a template filled from what was read, or an entry from a reviewed catalogue of errors and next steps, chosen by rules; every answer is checked by computation, never by a model.

Decision D96taken while writing, 29 October 2026; keeps D54's reason and supersedes its letter for Foundation

D54's reason was that a product which does not converse can take the generative model out altogether, and with it the gate, the latency and the chance of an unsupported sentence reaching a child. That still holds: the teacher reads only what someone wrote and someone else checked. What changed is that Foundation now needs models of a different kind, to see, and D96 says which kind. What it gives up is the same as before: a mistake the catalogue does not hold gets a plain description and no triage.

Where it runs

Three things are in the room: the desk units, the school's network and the teacher's PC. Foundation itself is software on the school's servers or in the cloud (D90), and the choice between them is the largest privacy difference in the design.

Desk unitA camera on a flexible arm, set at installation and locked, angled at the desk, sending frames over the school's network, encrypted, to the processing service. No screen, speaker, light or microphone (D90, T-FND-06).Nothing. It does not store frames.

Teacher's PCA Windows desktop app showing the class view and the term summaries (D92).Nothing on its disk: what it shows is fetched from the service and cleared when the app closes, so a shared or stolen PC holds no record.
  • On the school's servers, the processing service is a machine in the school, and no picture of a child leaves the building. The school then houses and powers it, and the company must support hardware it cannot reach.
  • In the cloud, frames cross the school's internet connection to the company's service and are discarded there. Support is simpler, and a school will ask where the service runs (Assumed OA-14).
  • Either way, the recognition models run inside Foundation's own service. No frame is sent to a third-party model vendor, whatever its retention terms, because D97 allows a frame no further than the service that reads it.

Which deployment comes first is not decided. How many frames a second are needed to follow a pencil stroke, and so the network and processing a whole class of desks needs, is Unknown until FM2 measures it; it may decide the question for most schools.

The page and hand reader

The reader turns each frame into text and throws the frame away. It runs in a fixed order, and the first step can stop it.

  1. Face check. Before anything else reads the frame, a face detector runs. A frame with any face in it is discarded before anything else reads it, whoever's face it is (T-FND-01). The detector is set to discard when unsure: a hand mistaken for a face costs a frame; a face mistaken for a hand would break a promise.
  2. The page. The reader finds the page and compares the frame with the last to find new strokes. It recognises them as characters: digits, letters, the operation signs, the equals sign, and the small carried digits of column working, each with its position on the page. Character by character, as it is written.
  3. The hand. A hand model places a skeleton of joints on the hand, as the film shows, and from it the reader records which fingers are on the pencil, the grip (tripod, fist or another listed form) and the pressure as far as it can be estimated, from the line and the posture of the fingers (OA-49).
  4. The reading. What survives is text: the desk, the time, the characters and their positions, the grip. The frame is then discarded (T-FND-02).

It reads what is there, not what was meant. A handwriting recogniser usually corrects towards real words; Foundation's must not, because the mistake is the point. No dictionary or language model corrects a spelling, a reversed letter or a missing capital. "i love my dog spike" is read as written.

Every reading carries a confidence, and a reading below the threshold raises no alert and says "unclear" on the tile, so that a misread digit never becomes a wrong answer the teacher is called over for. The threshold is set at FM2 from T-FND-04 and is Unknown today.

The models are bought or built, and learn only from consented data. No classroom frame is kept, so none can train a model (F3). Every model, and the data it learnt from, must be licensed for commercial use in a product for children. Which models, and whether any existing one reads a five-year-old's handwriting well enough, is Unknown and is the first question of FM2 (Assumed OA-48).

Arithmetic checks and wrong procedures

Every verdict on an answer is computed, and so is every diagnosis of a wrong answer: either the step where recomputing the working disagrees, or a wrong procedure that, run on the question, reproduces the child's answer.

  • The question comes from the page. For sums from the board, the child writes the question as well as the answer, and Foundation computes the answer to the question as written; it does not need to see the board. The teacher may type the board's sums when setting the lesson (D100), so that a miscopied question is noticed. For a task from the library, the task's specification holds the question.
  • The answer is computed, by the computer algebra check the Tutor uses, check (d) in 06, or by plain integer arithmetic where that is all it needs (T-FND-03). A model is never asked whether an answer is right.
  • The working is recomputed step by step. For a column sum, the reader has each column's digits and the carried digits; Foundation recomputes each column and names the first step that disagrees. In 16 + 8 + 19, the units make 23, so the carry is 2; a child who carried 1 is told apart from a child who added the tens wrongly.
  • A diagnosis is a reproduced answer. The error catalogue holds, for each kind of task, the known wrong procedures, each written as a computation and reviewed (F3). Foundation runs them on the question; a procedure that reproduces the child's answer selects its entry. For 4 × 6 = 16, adding (4 + 6 + 6) and counting in fours and stopping early both give 16, so the triage names both and suggests a step that tells them apart. If no procedure reproduces the answer, the tile says what was written and that it is wrong, and offers no guess.

A diagnosis is therefore never a model's opinion about a child, and it is worded as what the answer "can come from", because a reproduced answer shows a procedure is possible, not that the child used it. The teacher finds out which by asking.

The alert rules and the class view

The rules turn readings into the class view. They are the whole of Foundation's judgement, and each can be stated in a sentence a teacher would accept.

PartWhat it is
InputsThe readings from every desk; what the class is working on (D100); which desks are off (D99).
StateThis lesson's readings for each desk, and the term summary so far.
CatalogueReviewed entries for each kind of task: the wrong procedures, what each looks like in plain words, and a suggested next step; the writing marks to check; the grips to report (F3).
RulesWhen a tile turns amber: a wrong answer with no working; the same mistake twice; a mistake at the same step of the working; a long stretch with nothing written; a grip back to a fist. When a reading is only noted, in normal type, such as a sentence with its marks missing. When a tile goes quiet: the next answer is right, or the lesson ends. Thresholds such as how long counts as long are set with teachers in the pilot (OA-50).
OutputsFor each desk, a tile state and its words: a first line filled from a template ("Molly: 4 × 6 answered 16. No working shown."), and a catalogue entry if one was selected. The corner notice says only "Foundation", with one ping.

Every alert can be traced to the rule that raised it, the reading that triggered it and the entry that worded it, which matters to two readers. A teacher needs to trust an alert enough to walk over. And a school deciding whether to fit cameras in a Year 1 classroom needs to see that nothing in the product is a black box.

The class view never shows an image, because none exists (D97); never ranks the class (D98); and shows a desk whose camera is off as off (D99). If too many tiles turn amber at once, they are shown in the order they were raised, and the teacher is never shown a count of the class's mistakes. How many alerts a teacher can use in a lesson is Unknown until the pilot.

Cost

Cost per desk is not known. What can be said is where it falls.

Unknownmeasured at FM2 and FM3
  • Hardware, on every desk: the arm, the camera, and the fitting, so one unit for every child in the class, plus the teacher's PC if the school has none. Unknown per unit (OA-51).
  • Processing, continuously, for every desk with its camera on, for every lesson Foundation runs: either a machine in the school or a running cost in the cloud. Vision is far heavier than serving pages, and it is the cost the old design did not have. Unknown, measured at FM2.
  • Content: writing and reviewing the error catalogue and the task library (F3). Like the Tutor's examiner validation, it does not fall when the next model ships. Unknown, measured at FM2.
  • Training data: collecting children's writing and hands with consent, under the ethics protocol in F4. Unknown.
  • Installation and support: fitting units to desks, keeping them working, and the school's network. Unknown.
  • No generation cost: no model writes anything, so there is no cost per turn.

The consequence for the plan is the reverse of the earlier design's. Engineering effort spent reducing the processing per desk, the frame rate, how little of each frame is read, where the face check runs, is high-leverage for Foundation, because processing and hardware are the costs that grow with every desk. The numbers that decide whether Foundation is viable are the cost per desk, whether a school will pay it, and whether teachers use the alerts, and all three are in F6.
