Why an AI can do this now, and why at eight to thirteen

The Primer is built around what current AI does well; the Tutor is built around what it does badly. The Tutor's case rests on general models being unreliable about correctness, so the item bank and the verifier are the product (06). The Primer's rests on the opposite: holding a patient conversation, following a child's reasoning and asking the next good question is what language models do best. The owner's view is that this makes the Primer the lower-risk of the two new ideas on whether it can work at all. What limits it is content: every fact, figure and drawing it shows was written and checked by people first, so the catalogue, not the model, sets how much it can answer (OA-32, RP5).

That a child of 8 to 13 can hold a typed or spoken conversation on a screen long enough for an inquiry to work, and that this is something current AI does well. The youngest band is the least certain: a nine-year-old types slowly, is misheard more often and tires quickly. The two halves are tested at different times: whether a child can sustain the conversation is observed in a human-run pilot at PM3, before anything is built; whether an AI can lead it well is tested only when a built Primer runs at PM4.

AssumedOA-23, the owner's view

Speaking removes slow typing, which is why children may speak from launch (D102), ahead of counsel's answer on OI-17. It adds two things typing did not need: recognising a young child's speech, which is least accurate for the youngest Assumed (CV-11), and taking turns aloud in real time. That children speak readily, and are recognised well enough that the conversation does not stall, is Assumed (OA-55), tested at PM3 and PM4. The films set a brisk pace: one question takes three to six minutes, a design target (D112). The session cap stays a design choice set from PM3's pilot, and how soon an answer must begin is a threshold to be written down before PM4 (T-PRM-14), Unknown today.

The Primer still needs correctness. A child's why-question usually has a factual part before a philosophical one, and a confident wrong explanation to a nine-year-old is worse than none. So facts are held to the Tutor's rule: each cites a reviewed explanation and is blocked if unsupported (D44, T-PRM-01 in P3). A figure whose source is itself an estimate is said with "roughly" and keeps its source; a figure no reviewed entry holds is never said (D113, P3), so "roughly" is never a way round D44. Drawings are held to the same rule: each explainer is made in advance as part of a reviewed explanation, as D73 requires, and drawn by code from its reviewed specification, so that a child sees the same drawing every time and nothing is generated live by an image or video model; only its timing follows the voice (D111, P3). Conversation is where the model is trusted; facts, figures and drawings are where it is checked.

Eight is where the band starts, not where the ambition stops. Below about eight, the owner's view is that a screen alone cannot hold a child's attention or teach the physical side of reading and writing Assumed (OA-28), which is why Foundation puts no screen in front of the child at all (D93). From about eight, in the owner's judgement, a child can hold a conversation, and that is what the band rests on (D41); now that a child may speak, reading and typing no longer mark the floor. The band is a decision, not a finding: Decision D41 sets Foundation at 4 to 7, the Primer at 8 to 13 and the Tutor at 14 to 18.
