R1: a creator or board sues

Ranked first by the owner. The analysis in 04 says the ranking is right about the severity and wrong about the mechanism, and the correction matters more than the ranking.

You are very unlikely to be sued and quite likely to be switched off. An app store removes on a credible intellectual property complaint in days, sometimes hours, with limited process and no adjudication of the merits. A payment processor freezes on brand risk, often triggered by press rather than by a complainant. Neither reads the clean-room policy. Neither weighs the licence.

So the mitigation for R1 is not a stronger legal position. It is a prepared counter-notice pack, a second payment processor, and a web-first product that survives app store removal. All three are cheap, all three are at M1, and none of them is a legal argument.

R2: teaching something wrong before an exam

Ranked second, and it is the risk the architecture is mostly built around. A wrong answer three weeks before a GCSE is not a bug report; it is a harm to a specific child at the one moment the product exists to serve.

The residual, stated honestly: at the launch floor of 99.5% per turn, somewhere between one session in twenty and one in seven contains at least one unsupported assertion, the spread being a session length nobody has measured (03). That is the number to hold in mind, not the per-turn figure. D21 commits to reporting the per-session number and to withholding the "it does not make things up" claim until the per-turn floor reaches 99.9%.

Leading indicator: the weekly per-session groundedness figure, and the adjudication queue's composition. A rising share of failures concentrated in one topic is a content defect; a rising share spread evenly is a model or prompt regression.

What would make it worse: widening scope faster than validation capacity, which is the standard commercial pressure; or relaxing the gate to reduce refusals, which is the standard product pressure. Both are foreseeable and both would be rational in the moment.

R3: nobody pays

Ranked third. The most likely of the four to actually happen, and the cheapest to test.

  • The specific failure mode: parents anchor an AI tutoring subscription against software prices or against free, rather than against the verified £25 to £45 hourly tutoring rate. OA-10.
  • Leading indicator: landing-page conversion collapsing above a low price point. Detectable at M1, before anything is built.
  • Structural aggravator: the examination calendar caps retained months, so the denominator of the payback ratio has a ceiling nobody can lift (10). Price is anchored, cost is bounded, duration is capped. There is less room here than in a normal consumer subscription.
  • Second aggravator, self-inflicted and accepted: D25 forbids streaks, badges and return notifications, which are the mechanics every consumer subscription uses to defend retention. This decision costs real money and is made anyway.
  • Mitigation: test C1 and C2 at M1 for almost nothing; acquire Year 10 deliberately, where the affordable acquisition cost is roughly double; split CAC by channel so a creator-dependent business is visible rather than blended away.

Why engineering does not follow this ordering

The owner's ranking is a ranking of threats to the venture, and as such it is defensible. The engineering priority is R4, then R2, then R1, then R3, which is close to the reverse. Where the two orderings conflict inside a sprint, the engineering ordering wins.

DecisionStated as a decision so it cannot be treated as an oversight, 14 September 2026

Three reasons, and the first is the one that settles it.

  1. The harm in R4 is borne by someone who did not choose the risk and cannot recover from it. R1 and R3 cost the founders money and the investors a return. Both are voluntary participants in a risk they chose. A fifteen-year-old who discloses something to a tutor, or whose transcripts are breached, chose none of it and cannot be made whole afterwards. That asymmetry is not a commercial consideration and does not belong on the same scale as the others.
  2. R4's regulatory consequences are the only genuinely existential ones. Verified ICO to 4% of global turnover, Ofcom to 10% of qualifying worldwide revenue plus court-ordered business disruption reaching ISPs, app stores and payment providers. A creator dispute costs a preset and a bad week; an Ofcom disruption order costs the company. The owner's first-ranked risk is survivable and the fourth-ranked one is not.
  3. R4 failures are silent and compound; R1 and R3 failures announce themselves. Nobody sends a letter when a lawful basis is wrong. You find out at audit, years of processing later, with every day of it in scope. R3 announces itself in a conversion dashboard within a week.

The practical consequence: safety and data work is not scheduled after the features that address R1 and R3, and the DPIA is a precondition of launch rather than a launch task. If those two sentences ever stop being true in practice, this register has become decorative.

Which one actually breaks first

R3, and specifically the price anchor. It is the most likely to be true, it is the cheapest to discover, and it is discoverable before a single item is written.

The reasoning is not a probability estimate; it is about what is already known. R1, R2 and R4 are all managed by designs that exist in this repository and that have never been tested, so their real status is unknown rather than low. R3 is different: it rests on a single assumption, OA-10, that parents anchor against tutoring rates rather than software prices, and that assumption has no evidence behind it at all. Verified The competing anchor is free, in three products already on the parent's phone.

How it would be noticed: a landing-page conversion rate that falls off sharply above a low monthly price, at M1, in days, for a small spend. If it happens, the correct response is not to discount. It is to re-read the Route B case, because a parent market that will not pay tutoring-anchored prices is the strongest single argument for selling to institutions instead.
