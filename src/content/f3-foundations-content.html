Foundations: content and evidence

Everything Foundations tells an adult is written in advance by people who teach this age and checked by different people. Where a rule can check it, the words a child is shown, the order of new sounds and the sums, it also passes fixed rules that a model cannot override. The advice the adult reads aloud can be checked only by reviewers, the weakest check in the design. Whether guidance like this helps a child read is a separate question, and on that the evidence register is silent: this document says so, and says what would fill the gap.

The reading and number sequences

A sequence is the order in which a child meets new things: for reading, the correspondences between sounds and the letters that write them, then blending them to read words and splitting words into sounds to spell them; for number, counting, comparing and putting small numbers together and apart. Foundations' sequences are written originally, by our own writers, to the expectations English schools publicly work to.

In England, children take a phonics screening check in Year 1, and the Department for Education keeps a list of validated systematic synthetic phonics programmes, which start in Reception and give a structured route to the check. Schools are not required by law to choose a programme from the list.

AssumedCV-09, awaiting the owner's check

Foundations' reading sequence is written to that shape: sounds introduced one at a time in a planned order, practised by blending from the first week, with the common words that cannot yet be decoded taught as exceptions. It copies no programme's order, cards, names for sounds, actions, rhymes or characters, for the reason the Tutor's clean room gives in 03: a programme's materials are someone else's expression, and a product that borrows them is only as safe as the most protective publisher allows.

If any Department for Education material is used as a source for the sequences, the terms on which it may be used commercially are the question already put to counsel for the Tutor's subject content. Nothing here assumes the answer.

The clash with the school's order

This is the content problem most likely to make Foundations do harm, and it follows directly from the claim above. A child in Reception is being taught sounds by their school, in their school's order and with their school's names and actions for them. A home product that introduces sounds in a different order, or teaches a sound the class has not reached, competes with the teacher for the same small amount of a five-year-old's attention.

The design therefore makes two rules, recorded as decision D55. For a child in school, Foundations practises what the school has taught and does not teach ahead of it: the adult tells the product which sounds the class has covered, from what the school sends home, and a new correspondence is introduced only when the adult confirms the class has met it. For a child not yet in school, Foundations follows its own sequence. Whether adults can reliably tell what the class has covered is untested (OA-33); it is a question for parents and teachers in the field research guide, and if the answer is no, the first rule cannot work as written.

A tension, stated rather than hidden. D55's reason is that a home product which names sounds differently from the school competes with the teacher. But the clean room below keeps out every programme's names and actions for sounds, so Foundations' own words for a sound will often differ from the class's. The guide settles this in the school's favour: where the adult knows the school's words or actions for a sound, the guide tells them to use those, and it never presents its own as the right ones. The product holds nothing of the school's; the adult brings it, and types nothing in.

The sequence is enforced, not just written down: nothing is introduced out of order, and never more than one new thing in a session. That is T-FND-02, a fixed rule over the record, defined below.

Outside England

Decodable practice

A text is decodable, for a particular child, when every word in it can be read using only the correspondences that child has been taught, or is on the list of exception words for their stage. That is our definition, and it is the one the decodability rule enforces.

The design adopts the principle that early reading practice should use only what the child can decode, so that practice is decoding rather than guessing from pictures or context. It is the principle behind decodable practice texts generally; this vault adopts it, and does not prove it.

Assumeda teaching principle adopted, not evidenced here; see the evidence section

Practice texts are made in three ways, and all three meet the same gate:

  1. Word lists, generated from the correspondences taught by each step, filtered by writers for words a young child knows.
  2. Short phrases and sentences written by our writers for each step.
  3. Drafts proposed by a model, offline. A model may propose; only the rule and a reviewer may admit.

Every text passes the decodability rule before it enters the bank, and again before it is shown, against the actual child's record. A text that fails is never shown; the guide falls back to a stock text that passes. The rule is deterministic and runs on no model, for the reason the Tutor's mathematics check does: a check that shares a generator's judgement shares its mistakes (06). Floor: 100%, a design choice. Mechanism in F5.

Design choiceT-FND-01; the D27 reasoning, carried over

Two hard cases, named now rather than discovered later. A spelling with more than one pronunciation is checked against the reading the text intends, so the lexicon records intent as well as spelling. And names, which often do not decode, are excluded from practice texts or taught as exceptions, never slipped in.

Writing

Writing is where the owner's caution is sharpest, and the design takes it literally: the adult teaches writing; the screen teaches the adult how.

  • Forming letters. For each new letter, the guide shows the adult where the letter starts, which way the pencil travels and where it stops, and suggests forming it large in the air, in sand or salt on a tray, and then on paper, with the adult's hand guiding the child's where the child wants it.
  • Spelling. The adult says a word built from taught sounds; the child says the sounds and writes them; the adult compares what the child wrote with the word on screen and taps what happened.
  • Holding the pencil and sitting to write get short, reviewed guidance for the adult, who has no reason to know them without training.

The reason for doing it this way is the owner's view: at this age, forming letters by hand builds a link between hand and mind that a screen cannot, and the physical guidance is the teaching.

The owner's viewOA-28

Foundations never sees the child's writing. There is no camera and no handwriting recognition (D50); the adult judges, and the adult's judgement is the record. That costs accuracy, because an adult may accept a reversed letter the product would have caught, and it is the same trade D50 makes for reading.

Number

Number in Foundations is done with real objects, set out by the adult: buttons, pasta, spoons, steps across the kitchen. The screen never shows the child virtual objects to tap.

  • Counting, touching each object once, and knowing the last number said is how many there are.
  • Recognising small groups at a glance, without counting.
  • Comparing: more, fewer, the same.
  • Putting small numbers together and taking them apart: objects first, symbols later.

For each activity the guide tells the adult what to set out, what to ask, the answer to expect and what a typical wrong answer means. Every expected answer is computed, never generated, by the same checker the Tutor uses for its mathematics: T-FND-03, defined below and specified in F5.

The writer and the checker are never the same person, as in the Tutor's item bank. Every item carries its provenance: who wrote it, who checked it, when, against which version of the sequence, in the form 03 specifies for the Tutor.

The table has an uncomfortable column. For reading and number, much of the content can be checked by rules. For the guidance an adult actually reads, what to say to a five-year-old who is about to cry, nothing can be checked by a rule. That guidance is the part of Foundations most likely to matter and the part with the weakest check. The only test on it is whether untrained adults can follow it, which says nothing about whether it is good teaching.

Like the Tutor's examiner validation, this review is a rate-limited human process. Its cost is Unknown, measured at FM2 (F6).

What the evidence says, and what it does not

The evidence register holds nothing on how children of four to seven best learn to read, write or count, with software or without. That is the most important sentence in this section, and it is stated first so it cannot be missed.

What the register does hold, and what each item is worth here:

  • CV-09 says what English schools are expected to do: systematic phonics from Reception, towards a check in Year 1. That is policy. It tells Foundations what shape to fit; it is not evidence that any method works, and this vault has not read the evidence behind it. Assumed
  • CV-11 says speech recognition is least accurate for the youngest children. It supports D50, the decision not to listen; it says nothing about learning. Assumed
  • CV-04 is a novel, cited for its argument, never as evidence.
  • The evidence for AI tutoring in 00 concerns much older learners and does not transfer to a five-year-old.

So the design rests on the owner's view (OA-28), and it should not be presented as resting on anything stronger. The first evidence task is the cheapest one in the whole Foundations plan: read the published research on how early reading is taught, on home reading support delivered by parents, and on software for this age, before anything is built. That is reading, not building, and it sits in FM1 with the conversations in the field research guide. No figure from that literature appears in this document, because none has been read and registered, and the vault does not fill gaps from memory.

Two results would change the design. If the research shows that children of this age learn early reading well from software used alone, OA-28 is weakened, D42's first reason with it, and the owner should revisit both with a written reason. If it shows that guided home practice by untrained adults does little, Foundations' whole premise is weakened, and F6's decision rule should end it.

How we would know it works

Two different questions, and only the first can be answered by tests the product runs on itself.

Is the guidance correct? Where a rule can check it, the words, the order and the sums, the deterministic checks answer this on every release. The advice the adult reads is checked only by review, and T-FND-04 shows that it can be followed, not that it is good teaching (above).

Does the child learn? The product cannot answer this about itself, and should not be allowed to try.

  • The periodic unaided check in F2 carries D15's honesty to this age. But the adult reports it and wants the child to succeed, so it will flatter: useful for choosing the next step, worthless as proof.
  • Proof needs an independent measure: an assessor who is not the adult, using an established measure of early reading chosen with the evaluators at FM3. Which measure is Unknown until the research above has been read.
  • And a comparison, ideally against children whose adults spend the same time without the guidance, because the honest question is whether the guidance adds anything to the time. Designing that ethically, with children this young, needs an ethics protocol that does not yet exist (F4).

Until that evidence exists, Foundations can promise guidance that passes its checks and can be followed, and nothing more, which is what F2 promises.
