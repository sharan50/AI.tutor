Foundation: thesis

Foundation is a classroom instrument for the teacher of children of four to seven. A camera on a thin arm over each desk sees the page and the child's hand, never the face; Foundation reads what is written, whether it is right, where the working went wrong and how the pencil is held, and tells the teacher which child needs help, what the problem looks like and what to try. The child is shown nothing and told nothing. It is sold to schools first, and it does not go first of the three ideas: that is the owner's order, not a judgement that it matters less.

The claim, in four sentences

Foundation helps a teacher of children of four to seven see how every child in the class is doing, while the lesson is happening. A camera on every desk watches the page and the hand, and Foundation reads what is being written, whether the answer is right, where in the working a mistake was made and how the pencil is held. When a child needs help, the teacher's screen says who, what the problem looks like and what to try first, so the teacher walks over already knowing what to ask. The child never sees, hears or touches the product, and the teacher is always the one who acts.

Foundation is a purpose-built tool that helps teachers understand how the children in their class are doing. It is sold to schools first, as a tool for the teacher, not to parents and not for home use. It runs in the background of a normal lesson, and the teacher taps nothing during it. It covers maths, verbal and non-verbal reasoning, and motor skills.

Decision D89the owner's, 29 October 2026

The age band is unchanged: four to seven, Reception to Year 2 in England, set with the owner alongside the Primer's and the Tutor's (D41). What changed on 29 October 2026 is everything else. The earlier design was a screen that coached a parent or teaching assistant through a ten-minute session with one child, with no camera and no microphone, knowing only what the adult tapped. The owner replaced it entirely, and the ledger keeps it: each of the six decisions written for it carries a note on what supersedes it for Foundation.

Foundation's film on the front page shows the design: Miss Smith's Year 1 class, Molly's 4 × 6 = 16 with no working, the tile that turns amber, and the term summary at parents' evening. It is illustrative and made with AI; nothing in it has been built.

Why the teacher is the user

A teacher of a young class already knows what each child needs; what they cannot do is watch every page at once. Foundation does the watching and leaves the teaching where it is.

In a lesson, a child who writes 4 × 6 = 16 and moves on is invisible until the books are marked, by which time the lesson has passed and the child has practised the mistake. A child holding the pencil in a fist is noticed when the teacher happens to pass that desk. A child who has written nothing for a while may be thinking or may be stuck. These are the moments Foundation is for: it turns each one into a tile on the teacher's screen, with the detail and a suggested next step, while there is still time to help.

That makes the teacher the only user. The child has no account, no login and no screen, and is never addressed. The parent does not use Foundation either: they meet it through the teacher, at parents' evening, when the teacher opens a child's term summary. And the teacher is never replaced: Foundation says what it saw and what might help; the teacher decides whether it is right and what to do.

Foundation never talks to the child, never shows the child anything and never rewards the child: no stars, streaks, badges or confetti. It never points a camera or a microphone at a face. It never replaces the teacher, who is always the one who acts.

Decision D93the owner's, 29 October 2026; D25 already forbids the rewards

The owner's view of how children this age learn still holds, and now supports this rule instead of a home design: a five-year-old is taught by a person, physically and with attention, and a screen in front of the child does not help them (Assumed OA-28). Foundation puts no screen in front of the child at all. It gives the person who teaches them better eyes.

What it sees, and what it never sees

The camera is aimed at the desk: the page, the pencil and the hand. It is not aimed at the face, no face is read, and nothing it sees is kept.

A frame in which a face appears is discarded by a face check before anything else reads it; the check itself is the only processing such a frame meets. It keeps no image, video or audio: frames are read as they arrive and thrown away, and what is held is what was read, as text (D97). The desk unit has no screen, no speaker, no light and no microphone. How well an overhead camera can read the handwriting of a five-year-old, and the grip and pressure of their hand, is the first thing this design does not know (Assumed OA-48, OA-49).

What YC asked for, and where Foundation stands

Y Combinator's request for a Primer asks for adaptive teaching of reading, writing and arithmetic to young children, starting as something parents buy for basic skills, with the Primer of The Diamond Age as the long-term goal Assumed CV-01.

As secondary write-ups report it, the request asks for teaching close to a private tutor's quality at consumer scale, supplementing teachers rather than replacing them, for ages four to ten.

AssumedCV-01, awaiting the owner's check; the primary page could not be opened from the build environment

Foundation answers the same need from the other side. It does not teach the child, and it is not bought by parents. It strengthens the teacher who already teaches the child, in the place where basic skills are taught, and it is bought by the school. That keeps the part of the request the owner agrees with, supplementing teachers. It departs from the request where the owner's view does: Foundation puts nothing in front of the child (OA-28), and it is bought by schools, not parents. An application to YC should say so plainly, and should also say that Foundation is not the idea that goes first.

The strongest case against this position deserves stating, because a reviewer will make it. A product a parent can buy tonight reaches a family faster than one a school must approve, install and fund, and a school's purchases may follow the academic year, so that a missed decision costs a year (Assumed OA-12 sets the same expectation for the Tutor's route through schools). The owner's answer is that the school is where these skills are taught, and a tool for the teacher reaches every child in the class, not only those whose parents buy it.

Why it does not go first

Decision D94the owner's, 29 October 2026

The reasons D42 gave no longer describe this product. It said a young child needs a person, and Foundation now has the teacher in the room. It said schools already teach this for free, and Foundation is now a tool for that teaching, which the school does not already have. It said the risk of not working was the highest of the three, a judgement made about a screen coaching a parent. None of that is restated here as a new reason, because the owner gave none: the order is the owner's, and the design is how the owner has said Foundation will be built when its turn comes. F6's rule, proposed for the owner, says what evidence that build should wait on.

Some things will make Foundation slower when it comes, and they belong in the plan, not in the reason for the order: hardware on every desk, a school's due diligence on a camera in a Year 1 classroom, and two counsel questions that did not exist for the home design (F4, F6).

Where the case is weak

In order of how much each would matter if it went wrong.

  • A camera on every desk of a Year 1 class. Whatever it is pointed at, it is a camera in a room of five-year-olds, and the questions it raises are open: the lawful basis, whether a hand's pose or a child's handwriting read this way is biometric data, and what children this young must be told (Open OI-28; OI-29 in the US). Parents and staff may refuse it however it is designed (Assumed OA-52).
  • Nobody knows whether it can read a five-year-old's page. Handwriting at this age is large, uneven and often reversed; grip is visible from above, pressure much less so. If the reading is poor, every alert is noise (OA-48, OA-49).
  • Alerts may be a burden. A teacher with a whole class may not want a screen calling them during a lesson, and too many alerts would be worse than none (Assumed OA-50).
  • Hardware on every desk is a cost the Tutor does not have, and one a school carries, where parents buy the Primer's tablet. Who in a school would pay for it, and how much, is Unknown (OA-51).
  • The register holds no evidence that seeing a mistake sooner helps a child of this age. The design assumes that a teacher who knows sooner helps sooner, and that helping sooner matters. Neither is evidenced here (F3).
  • It has had one independent review. The Tutor's documents needed eight adversarial rounds to find what was wrong with them.

None of these is hidden, because the purpose of these documents is to let the owner decide on evidence how Foundation is built when its turn comes. F6 says what would justify building it and what would stop it.
