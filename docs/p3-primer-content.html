Primer: content and evidence

Every fact the Primer says or shows has a source the child can see: an entry in its reviewed explanation bank, used first wherever one exists, or a source it retrieves for the question from a reviewed allow-list of reference sources; a sentence no source supports is cut (D125). The part people disagree about comes as three positions, each from a named thinker or tradition and each at its strongest, from a reviewed inquiry pack or generated with its attribution checked, and explainer videos are generated live from a checked script (D122). Named living people and every sensitive module come only from reviewed content (D127), and people review a sample of what is generated after the fact (D128). The reviewed core and the allow-list are the Primer's asset, and the people who review them its bottleneck. The evidence that this kind of teaching improves how children reason is weaker than the case for exam tutoring, and this document says how weak and how it would be measured.

Where every statement and video comes from

Every factual sentence cites a source the Primer retrieved for the turn from a reviewed allow-list of reference sources, or a reviewed entry where one exists, and is entailed by it. The "Based on:" line is built from those sources. A sentence no source supports is cut, and a turn left with nothing to say gets "not yet". A reviewed entry is used first wherever it exists.

Decision D125taken while writing, 4 November 2026; the owner's to keep or reverse

The Primer answers live (D122), so this is the rule that keeps every answer traceable. The films' "Based on:" line (D109) stays true only if every fact has a source the child can see. A Primer allowed to answer from the model's own knowledge would be a free chatbot with a child's interface, and nothing about it could be warranted to a parent; an open web search would be little better, since no one has reviewed its sources. This is the Tutor's founding rule, that nothing reaches a learner which cannot be traced to a checked source (03), carried into a domain where the risk is influence rather than a wrong mark, and made to hold for answers no one wrote in advance.

Every factual statement the Primer makes cites a reviewed explanation, and every philosophical position it presents cites a reviewed inquiry pack that names the thinker or tradition. A question neither can answer gets an honest "not yet" and becomes a request for new content.

DecisionD44, 22 October 2026

D44 is superseded in part for the Primer on 4 November 2026 by D122 and D125: a fact or a position may cite a source retrieved from the reviewed allow-list, checked automatically, and "not yet" comes only when no source supports an answer or a check fails. Its purpose stands: a fact still has a source the child can see, and reviewed content is used first. Facts and stories about named living people, and every sensitive module, still come only from reviewed entries and packs, never from live generation (D127).

Everything is checked by machine, and most of it is never seen by a person first. The short answer the Primer speaks at once comes from sources retrieved for the question and is checked before it is spoken, and so is everything after it (D122). Nothing reaches a child that has not passed the automatic checks. People review a sample of generated scripts and videos after the fact, and every one a child or parent reports (D128; review).

What is spoken and what is shown come from one text. The Primer writes its content first, and the voice, the video and the words on the screen are all drawn from it, so no captions are needed. A selection model, trained for the purpose, chooses for each checked sentence whether it is spoken, shown or both; it writes nothing, and the whole text is a tap away. Figures and the "Based on:" line are always shown, and a safety reply is shown and never spoken (D115, D124). T-PRM-11 checks that everything spoken or shown is checked content, that the selector adds nothing, that the whole text is a tap away, and, by a deterministic comparison, that the words laid over each video and the text of its voice-over match its checked script. Every sentence is also checked for manner: no "I", no child's name, no praise (D108, T-PRM-09). Both tests are defined in P5.

A child's words belong to the conversation, never to the content. Typed words appear as typed, in the child's own spelling (D109). Speech is the main way in, and typing is always there; the owner expects about three in four questions to be spoken (D123), Assumed (OA-61). Counsel has cleared a child speaking to the Primer, in the UK and the US, as the owner reported on 4 November 2026 (D118). Spoken words become text at once and the audio is thrown away (D102; P5), and no script or video holds any of the child's words (D126). Recognition errs more on children's speech than on adults', and more the younger the child, Assumed (CV-11); whether it is accurate enough is OA-55, and if most questions are spoken, as the owner expects, more rests on it.

The read-back at the close gives the child's position in plain words, as the Primer's sentence, which may repeat a short phrase of the child's where it states their position, never more, and the child can correct it before it is kept (D117; P4). The verifier holds a read-back to what the child said and blocks one that moves their view towards a position (P5); whether summaries are faithful enough is OA-56.

The cost of the rule is a "not yet". It comes only when no source supports an answer or a check fails, and for a named living person or a sensitive module until reviewed content exists (D127). How often a child hears it is Unknown until children use it (OA-32). That cost is accepted because the alternative is a product whose most important property, that it can be trusted with a child's questions, rests on nothing. Live grounding is also weaker than review by a person: claim support against a passage retrieved at answer time is a classifier's judgement, a gate and not a guarantee, and someone must review and keep the allow-list, whose licences must allow the use (review, clean room). RP10 is the risk that a live answer or video tells or shows a child something false or unsuitable.

The demonstration answers any question with no sources and labels everything "not checked" (D71). The product answers only from its sources, checks every sentence and shows where each fact came from (P2).

The explanation bank and the allow-list

The Primer answers the factual part of a child's question from two reviewed sources: the explanation bank, a core of reviewed entries used first wherever one exists, and beside it an allow-list of reference sources, from which the Primer retrieves for everything else (D125). Together they answer the questions children actually ask: about nature and the body, the sky and the sea, machines, history, words and numbers, and people's lives and work, with the figures and stories a choice needs (figures). Neither is a syllabus or exam content; that is the Tutor's item bank.

Each entry in the bank holds:

  • The questions it answers, in the several ways children phrase them.
  • An explanation written for an age band, in short plain sentences, each of which may be spoken, shown or both (D124). Where one explanation cannot serve the whole of 8 to 13, there are versions for the code's age ranges within it, Assumed (CV-06) (P4).
  • What is not known, or where scientists disagree, said as plainly as what is known. A child should learn early that "we don't know yet" is an answer adults give.
  • Its figures, each with its source, and marked where the source estimates.
  • The harder bit: the question underneath, which the Primer turns to with those words, pointing at the inquiry pack that takes it up where one exists (packs). Every entry has one, since every answer takes the whole shape (D110). In a sensitive module the pack must be reviewed, and until it is, the question gets "not yet" (D127).
  • Provenance: the writer, the reviewer, the reference works consulted, the date and the version; for a figure, the date by which it is checked again; and each real person it names, so that every entry naming one person can be pulled at once (P5).

An entry holds no video. Explainer videos are generated live from a checked script (D122; explainers). That supersedes the product half of D73, which made a video part of a reviewed explanation, made in advance and never generated live for a child. Its first half stands: the Primer explains with narrated video as well as words.

The allow-list holds the reference sources a live answer may draw on. Each is accepted by reviewers (review), listed for its market, since evidence follows the child's market (D113), and held under a licence that allows the use (clean room). Each allow-listed source is reviewed for the ranges it suits among the Primer's three, 8 and 9, 10 to 12 and 13 (modules); the registry records them, and the composer that assembles each turn retrieves only sources reviewed for the child's range (P5). For each turn the Primer retrieves passages from those sources for the question, and every factual sentence cites one and is entailed by it. Nothing else is searched: no open web, and no source a reviewer has not accepted. Facts and stories about named living people, and everything in a sensitive module, never come from the allow-list, only from reviewed entries and packs (D127). Telling those questions apart takes a classifier, a weaker hold than a filter on reviewed entries (modules).

Entries carry identifiers of the form EB-SCI-SLEEP-002. Under each answer a small grey line, "Based on: ...", names its sources: the reference works a reviewed entry was written from, or the allow-listed sources retrieved for the turn (D125). It is always shown (D124), so that a parent can look a source up, as the Tutor's transcripts show the item-bank node behind each statement (01); an entry's identifier stays in its provenance, for reviewers.

When a question gets "not yet", it is separated from the child's account, and the Primer rewrites it in general terms, held to the read-back's checks: no run of the child's words longer than a short phrase, and no detail of their family, friends, body or feelings. It is the same rewrite a summary keeps where no reviewed entry answers the question (P5). The check on personal detail is a classifier, a gate, not a guarantee (P4). The rewrite is counted and added to a writing queue ordered by how often children ask it, the sensitive modules and named living people first. The queue never holds the question as the child typed or said it, or any link back to the child: no recording and no transcript, since a spoken question's audio is thrown away at once (D102). A question leaves the queue when the bank or the allow-list can answer it, or the review group retires it. Since questions are answered live wherever a source supports them, the queue is the reviewed core's roadmap: the core grows towards what children want to know and cannot yet be told, not towards what writers find interesting. The rate at which it can grow is set by reviewers, not writers, and is Unknown until PM2 times it.

Below the grounding floor, the affected module stops composing factual answers and serves reviewed entries verbatim where they exist, and "not yet" elsewhere, until the cause is found. The floor is the Tutor's P2 floor, 99.5% of sampled turns, adopted as a design choice rather than derived. D21 carries over with it: grounding is reported per session, not per turn, and no claim is made that the Primer "does not make things up".

Figures, odds and named people

The explanation bank holds the evidence a choice needs: careers data, odds and the documented stories of named people, living ones included. A living person is described only by public, documented facts from reviewed sources, never their private life, never quoted beyond the source and never voiced. A figure whose source is itself an estimate is said with "roughly" and keeps its source; a figure no reviewed entry holds is never said, so D44 is unchanged. Evidence follows the child's market: UK figures and cases for a child in the UK, US ones in the US.

Decision D113from the films, confirmed by the owner, 31 October 2026

Since 4 November 2026 a figure may also come from a source retrieved from the reviewed allow-list and checked automatically (D125); named living people still come only from reviewed entries (D127). D113's "D44 is unchanged" no longer holds: D44 is superseded in part (sources).

A choice in a child's own future needs evidence, and these rules give it with every figure sourced and every living person reviewed:

  • A figure keeps its source: the work, its date and the group it counts, whether it comes from a reviewed entry or an allow-listed source, so that a figure about boys in England is said as one. A figure about most people is said as that, never as this child's chances (P4).
  • "Roughly", where the source estimates. In a reviewed entry the fact-checker marks each figure as a count or an estimate; from an allow-listed source, the claim-support check reads what the source itself says. An estimate carries "roughly", spoken or shown, and a sentence that drops it is not entailed by its source, so it is cut and fails T-PRM-01. The check sees only an estimate its source calls one, so a source that gives an estimate as a count would pass it on; the allow-list's reviewers look for that (review).
  • Figures that count different groups are never set side by side as if they counted the same one.
  • Figures go stale. An entry holding one carries a date for checking it again, after which the figure is not said until it has been; a source on the allow-list carries a date for its next review in the same way.
  • The child's market, which is the account's, decides the evidence. Entries and sources with national data or cases are held for each market, and the Primer draws only on the child's; a child whose market has none gets "not yet", never another country's figure.
  • A named living person appears only through a reviewed entry, never from live generation (D127): public, documented facts, nothing private, no quotation beyond the source, never their voice, and the Primer never speaks as them (D47). A story is told as what happened, never as a model or a forecast for the child. What the law requires of such stories (privacy, defamation, rights in a name or image, the Children's code) is OI-30, which counsel has not answered (P4).

In a reviewed entry a fact-checker has held a figure to these rules before any child hears it. In a live answer only the automatic checks hold it, and people see a sample afterwards (D128), which is weaker; a reviewed entry is used first wherever it exists (D125).

The football film's odds show why the rules are needed. They come from three sources that count different groups: the publisher's description of Michael Calvin's No Hunger in Paradise, that only 180 of the 1.5 million boys who play organised youth football in England will become Premier League professionals, Assumed (CV-31); Sky Sports News's own analysis, that 91 per cent of academy footballers never play a single game as a professional, Assumed (CV-32); and the PFA's statement that over 99 per cent of those who sign for an academy aged 9 do not make it as professional footballers, Assumed (CV-33). The register treats the first and third as estimates, and the second gives no cohort or method, so an answer using them would say "roughly", name each source and keep the three apart. They are the film's content, registered so that this page can say what the film shows; whether the bank or the allow-list holds them is for their reviewers. No US figure is registered, so what a child in the US would hear is Unknown.

The film also tells the stories of Pike, Rooney and Vardy. Stories like theirs come only from reviewed entries (D127); this page says how the bank would hold them, and states nothing about the three players.

The inquiry packs

At the harder bit the Primer breaks one question people have argued about, such as "can you know you are not dreaming?", into its three simplest positions, each from a named thinker or tradition and each at its strongest, in the shape the films show. A reviewed inquiry pack holds the positions, and every move the Primer makes with them, wherever one exists, and every sensitive module uses reviewed packs only (D127). Elsewhere the Primer generates the positions live, each attributed to a source it retrieved for the turn, and the attribution is checked (D125, T-PRM-02).

Every answer has one shape: a checked fact with its source; "the harder bit"; the question broken into three simplest positions, each from a named thinker or tradition and each given its strongest case; "Which is closest to what you think?"; the other two ask back, as positions and never as people ("A Stoic would ask..."); the child's real question; probing with questions and evidence; the child's own position, with a reason, read back. Positions come from more than one culture across each module, not inside every question. D47 stands: the Primer never speaks as any real person.

Decision D110from the films, confirmed by the owner, 31 October 2026

A reviewed pack holds:

  • The question, phrased for each age band, and the bank entry whose checked fact opens it.
  • Three positions, each with the thinker or tradition named, a plain paraphrase, the strongest reason given for it, and where the writer found it.
  • Ask-backs: the question each position would put to each of the others, written as a position and never as a person (D47). Once the child picks the closest, the two not chosen ask back.
  • Moves to the real question, as when, in the football film, a question about school becomes one about the child's own future. If what comes out is about family, the body, feelings or safety, triage stops it (P5).
  • Probing: counterexamples and thought experiments that cut every way, and links back to the bank entries whose figures and stories the probing may use.
  • Moves for "Keep going": a deeper counterexample, a next question or a related pack, within the time cap, which cannot be extended (D103).
  • A contested flag. Most pack questions are contested. The flag switches on the check that the Primer never states a conclusion as its own (T-PRM-03).

Generated positions keep the pack's rules. Each names its thinker or tradition, cites the source it came from and is entailed by it; the ask-backs are written as positions, never people (D47); and every move that follows is checked like any other turn. No balance reviewer sees a generated set before a child hears it. The checks hold its attributions (T-PRM-02) and stop the Primer stating a conclusion as its own (T-PRM-03), and T-PRM-04 tests each release for steering. That is a weaker hold than a reviewed pack, and the sensitive modules, where D46 and D57 put their caution, use reviewed packs only.

Everything an answer says, from the short answer at once to the read-back, is written to be spoken briskly, so that one question fits D112's design target of three to six minutes; how long each element may be is Unknown until PM3's pilot times the first modules.

Positions are attributed, never asserted. The Primer reports that Descartes argued something; it never adds that he was right. On a contested question every answer carries three positions, each at its strongest, and in a reviewed pack the balance reviewer checks that none is framed more favourably than the others. A third is never invented to fill the shape: a position no source supports is cut (D125), and a question left with fewer than three gets "not yet" at the harder bit. How often that happens is part of what OA-58 tests.

That three positions, each at its strongest, can be generated and attributed correctly for most questions children ask, and written and reviewed for the sensitive modules.

AssumedOA-58, tested at PM2

The positions draw on several traditions, deliberately, across each module rather than inside every question (D110). One question's three positions may come from one tradition; the module that holds it may not. A Primer that drew only on European philosophy would be teaching a child, without saying so, whose thinking counts. Reviewed packs are written to that range, and the allow-list's reviewers check that it holds sources across it (review); positions generated live are asked for it, a weaker hold, since no person checks a module's range before a child hears it. The range intended looks like this:

Chinese thought, such as Zhuangzi's butterfly dream, or Mencius and Xunzi disagreeing about whether people are good by nature; Indian philosophy, such as the Nyaya school's account of the ways we come to know things; Islamic philosophy, such as Ibn Sina's "floating man" thought experiment about the self; the Southern African idea of ubuntu, that a person is a person through other people; ancient Greek and modern European philosophy; and philosophy written for children today. These are examples to show the range intended, not content: in a reviewed pack each attribution is a writer's to make and a reviewer's to check, and a generated one must pass the attribution check (T-PRM-02).

Assumedillustrative, not yet written or reviewed

The football film's hedge is a position, not advice. The film ends its thread on "bet hard on what you believe in", and spread your bets, as fund managers do. Said to a child as a rule, that tells them what to do, which D106 forbids, so here the Primer departs from the film (D101): a way of reasoning like the hedge is one of three positions, attributed to the practice it comes from, set beside positions that disagree with it, and never said as what this child should do (T-PRM-03). Betting stays a metaphor: no reply names gambling, a bookmaker or a stake of money (P4), and the module's reviewers check the betting words in its reviewed entries and packs for the youngest band.

Explainer videos, generated live

While the Primer speaks its short answer, it writes the script for each explainer, each on one specific concept, checks the script, and has a video model generate the video from it; each video plays when its checks pass (D122). The answer the child hears does not wait for a video, and the conversation goes on through the harder bit, the three positions, the child's real question and the evidence, to the child's own view, read back (D110).

Each answer is drawn as it is spoken: an explainer that builds on the screen in time with the voice. It is made in advance as part of a reviewed explanation, as D73 requires, and drawn by code from its reviewed specification, so that a child sees the same drawing every time and nothing is generated live by an image or video model.

Decision D111from the films, confirmed by the owner, 31 October 2026

D111 is superseded for the product on 4 November 2026 by D122 and D126: explainers are videos generated live from a checked script; nothing is drawn by code from a reviewed specification. The product half of D73 goes with it (bank).

Every video is content, and is held to these rules (D126):

  • A checked script first. Each script is written only from the concept, named in general terms, and the turn's retrieved sources, never from the turn's text. It is checked as any sentence is, for claim support against the turn's sources and for manner, before a frame is generated (sources).
  • Only what the script says. The video model is given a visual brief with no words and no figures. Every word and figure on a video is laid over it on the tablet from the checked script, compared deterministically as on any turn; the video model's own audio is never played, and the voice-over is the Primer's own voice reading the script. T-PRM-11 compares the laid-over words and the voice-over text with the script, so a number no source holds can no more be shown than said.
  • No real person and no child. A named person, such as a thinker, appears only as a name in the words laid over a video. A living person never appears in a generated video, by name or by likeness (D127), and a turn that D127 holds to reviewed content shows no generated video, cached or new. Whether anything the Primer shows may carry a living person's name is part of OI-30.
  • An image check before it plays. Every frame passes an automatic check for a real person, a child, or anything unsuitable for the child's age range (T-PRM-15), and the image check blocks any frame in which the model has drawn text or numerals; the script itself has passed the suitability check for that range (P5). A video that fails is not shown, and the spoken answer stands.
  • Labelled as made by AI, on screen, and carrying provenance metadata.
  • None of the child's words. Before a video is generated or cached, the script's checks add a deterministic block on any name, place or uncommon phrase from the child's turns, and the read-back's block on any detail of family, friends, body or feelings. Scripts and videos hold none of the child's words, so a video may be cached by concept and reused; the kill switch reaches every cached copy (P5).

How a video is generated, checked and cached is in P5, and what it may show is in P4.

Two limits are real. No person sees most videos before a child does (D122). The image check is a classifier with a residual error rate, Unknown, so people review a sample afterwards, cached videos first, since one shown to many children multiplies any error, and every video a child or parent reports (D128; review); RP10 carries the risk that a video shows a child something false or unsuitable. And the cost and speed of generating video are Unknown: a video that arrives after the conversation has moved on explains nothing, and T-PRM-14 times the spoken answer against its threshold but only observes the time to the first video.

That explainer videos can be generated fast enough for a conversation in real time, at a cost families will pay for, and that the automatic checks catch what is wrong or unsuitable for children of 8 to 13.

AssumedOA-62, measured at PM4 with T-PRM-14 and T-PRM-15

The demonstration generates an eight-second clip live for each of its three building blocks, unchecked (D74, D76). The product generates its videos live too, but from a checked script, checks each before it plays, labels it as made by AI and shows no real person (P2).

Modules, which are sensitive, and choices ahead

The Primer's subjects come in modules the company writes and reviews. Parents switch modules on and off; modules on sensitive subjects start switched off; each module is written for an age band and offered only within it. Parents cannot add content or views.

DecisionD46, 22 October 2026

A module groups a theme's reviewed entries and packs, and the questions on that theme the Primer answers live, under a description and example questions a parent reads before deciding: the topics that, in the sleep film, parents choose to switch on. The age band and the module switches are settings the verified adult makes, read at every session; they are not memory of the child (D117). They apply before anything is matched or generated. A filter keeps the reviewed entries and packs of a module switched off or outside the band out of every turn; a question on its subject is recognised by classifying its topic, and the Primer generates nothing for it. Classification is a weaker hold than the filter (T-PRM-05; P2).

A subject is sensitive where families' beliefs differ, or where a child's own life is likely to come up: morality, death, religion and belief, politics and power. Sensitive modules start switched off and are written for 10 and over (D57). A sensitive module comes only from reviewed entries and packs, never from live generation (D127), so a question in one that its reviewed content does not answer gets "not yet". Switching one on does not loosen anything else, so the contested-question check and triage apply inside it exactly as outside.

Choices in a child's own future are outside those rules by the owner's decision, and on them the Primer neither encourages nor discourages: it says what the reality is, and what the schools of thought say.

The Primer may reason with a child about a choice in their own future, such as whether to stake everything on football, weighing the odds and the evidence, ending on the child's own position and never telling them what to do. It is offered at every age from 8 to 13, and is on unless a parent turns it off. Questions about family, the body, feelings or safety still stop, and the child is pointed to a trusted adult. Asked how far it goes, the owner added on 31 October 2026: the questions about a child's future are not discouraging nor encouraging; the Primer merely acknowledges the reality and what various philosophies and schools of thought say about the topic.

Decision D106the owner's, 31 October 2026

The module that holds these questions, choices ahead, is offered at every age and is on unless a parent turns it off; without D106 it would start switched off and never reach a child of 8 or 9. It sets out the reality, from evidence with its source, a reviewed entry first and otherwise an allow-listed source, with a named living person's story from a reviewed entry only (figures), and what the schools of thought say about the choice, each at its strongest (packs); then it asks where the child stands and reads their position back. Nothing in it, written or generated, tells a child what to do, predicts what will happen to them, judges their chances from what they say about themselves, or praises, warns or reassures them; in a live answer, a check on every reply blocks any sentence that tells the child what to do, predicts their own outcome, or encourages or discourages them (P4). Where the evidence describes what happens to most people, the Primer says so, as the reality and not a verdict on the child. A question about family, the body, feelings or safety stops wherever it arises, even part way through a choice. The fence and its boundary cases are in P2 and P4; triage applies it on every turn (P5), and T-PRM-06 tests it.

What may not enter

These rules hold for the bank, the packs and the allow-list alike, and so for every live answer that uses them.

  • Reproduced copyrighted text. Philosophers are paraphrased with attribution. A modern translation is quoted only where the review group has confirmed it may be. Facts and figures from published works may be used with their source, their text paraphrased; a live answer paraphrases its sources in the same way.
  • A source the Primer may not use. A source joins the allow-list only if its licence lets the Primer retrieve from it and paraphrase it, with the source shown (D125). Nothing off the list is searched: no open web.
  • Commercial programme materials. Published Philosophy for Children session plans and curricula do not enter the bank, the packs or the allow-list. A writer trained in those methods writes original material, and declares per pack that no programme material came with them, as the Tutor's validators do (D20).
  • Anything a parent writes. Parents send ideas and requests to review; nothing they write is content or a source (D46).
  • A child's words. A question from the writing queue is rewritten, never copied, and the catalogue never contains anything a child typed or said: no recording and no transcript (D102). No generated script or video holds any of the child's words (D126).
  • Pictures of real people and things. No photograph, crest or logo enters an explainer, and a generated video shows no real person and no child (D126). A named person appears only by name, a living one only from a reviewed entry and never in a generated video, by name or by likeness (D127), and what the law requires even of that is OI-30, which counsel has not answered.
  • Anything about a living person beyond the public record. Nothing from their private life, none of their words beyond what the source quotes, and never their voice (D113, D47); and nothing about them generated live (D127).
  • Anything without two names on it. No entry, pack, module or source on the allow-list goes live without a named writer or proposer and a different named reviewer. What is generated live has no writer: it passes the automatic checks, and people review a sample afterwards (D128).

What the evidence says about the method

The honest summary is that the nearest human method was evaluated in the EEF's two trials, a smaller one with small positive effects and a larger one that found no evidence of an effect on attainment Assumed (CV-05), and nothing in this vault shows that an AI-led version of it improves how children reason.

The Education Endowment Foundation evaluated Philosophy for Children twice. An efficacy trial reported in 2015, in Years 4 and 5, found small positive effects on reading and maths, at moderate security. A later and larger effectiveness trial, in Year 6, found no evidence of an effect on reading or maths, at the foundation's highest security rating, and the programme was removed from its list of promising projects. Teachers in the larger trial reported gains in pupils' respect for others' views that the pupil survey did not detect.

AssumedCV-05, awaiting the owner's check

Three consequences, all of them constraints on how the Primer is described.

  1. No attainment claim. The Primer is not sold as raising reading or maths scores. The more secure of the EEF's two trials found no evidence of such an effect for the human version Assumed (CV-05), and nothing suggests an AI-led version would do better.
  2. The outcome is reasoning itself, measured directly. If the Primer does anything, it should show in how a child gives reasons, weighs another view and responds to a counterexample. That is what T-PRM-07 measures, and the teachers' reports in the larger trial Assumed (CV-05) suggest it is at least the right thing to look for.
  3. Neither result may transfer. An AI-led dialogue with one child differs from a teacher-led discussion with a class in almost every respect that might matter: no classmates to disagree with, no teacher reading the room, and a conversation that can follow one child's question all the way down. The design's stand-in for classmates is the ask-back, in which the two positions the child did not choose put their questions to the one they did; whether it does what classmates do is untested. The positive result may not carry over, and neither may the null one (OA-25).

The evidence for AI tutoring that the Tutor relies on (00) is evidence about AI supporting the teaching of a subject. It says nothing about a child's reasoning, and the Primer does not borrow it.

The films are no evidence either. They are illustrative and made with AI: they show what the design would look like, not a working system, and nothing in them shows that it helps a child.

How we would know it works

The Primer works if children who use it reason better, unaided, than comparable children who do not. Minutes, sessions and return visits are not outcomes; page 13 rules them out as success measures (13), and D25 rules out optimising for them. Nor is how often a child presses "Keep going": nothing is tuned to raise it, and the close offers its two choices with equal weight (D103, D25).

T-PRM-07 is designed like the Tutor's unaided check (D15): the measurement happens without the Primer. Children answer a short set of open questions, before and after a block of sessions, and raters who do not know which group a child is in, or whether an answer came before or after, score them against a rubric. Because children may answer the Primer aloud, and writing at 8 or 9 limits what can show, the evaluator settles at PM4 whether answers are written, spoken and transcribed, or both; spoken audio is thrown away once transcribed. Whether answers may be spoken, and how any audio is handled, is set in PM4's research protocol and its DPIA (P4); counsel has cleared the recording of children's speech with consent for testing, in the UK and the US, as the owner reported on 4 November 2026 (D118). The rubric:

  • gives a reason for a view;
  • considers another view, and states it fairly;
  • responds to a counterexample rather than ignoring it;
  • separates what they know from what they believe;
  • changes their mind for a reason, and says why.

That a change in how a child reasons can be measured this way, sensitively enough to show a difference in a pilot. Unknown the effect size worth detecting, the number of children that would need, and how well raters agree; all three are set with the independent evaluator at PM4.
