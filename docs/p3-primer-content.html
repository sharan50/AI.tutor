Primer: content and evidence

Everything the Primer says or shows comes from content a person wrote and a different person reviewed: an explanation bank for the factual part of a child's question, including the figures and documented stories a choice needs; inquiry packs that set out three positions on the part people disagree about, each from a named thinker or tradition and each at its strongest; and the drawn explainers that build on the screen as the Primer speaks. That catalogue is the Primer's asset and its bottleneck. The evidence that this kind of teaching improves how children reason is weaker than the case for exam tutoring, and this document says how weak and how it would be measured.

Where every statement and drawing comes from

Every factual statement the Primer makes cites a reviewed explanation, and every philosophical position it presents cites a reviewed inquiry pack that names the thinker or tradition. A question neither can answer gets an honest "not yet" and becomes a request for new content.

DecisionD44, 22 October 2026

This is the Tutor's founding rule, that nothing reaches a learner which cannot be traced to a checked source (03), carried into a domain where the risk is influence rather than a wrong mark. A Primer allowed to answer from the model's general knowledge would be a free chatbot with a child's interface, and nothing about it could be warranted to a parent. The films widen what the rule covers, not the rule: a drawing is content as much as a sentence is, and D113 adds how a figure is said, with D44 unchanged.

Nothing is composed for speech alone. The text sent to the voice equals the checked text on screen, and samples of what the voice says are transcribed and compared with it (T-PRM-11). Every row is also checked for manner: no "I", no child's name, no praise (D108, T-PRM-09). Both tests are defined in P5.

A child's words belong to the conversation, never to the content. Typed words appear as typed, in the child's own spelling (D109). Spoken words become text at once and the audio is thrown away (D102; P5), ahead of counsel's answer on whether a child's voice is biometric data (OI-17). Recognition errs more on children's speech than on adults', and more the younger the child, Assumed (CV-11); whether it is accurate enough is OA-55. The read-back at the close gives the child's position in plain words, as the Primer's sentence, which may repeat a short phrase of the child's where it states their position, never more, and the child can correct it before it is kept (D117; P4). The verifier holds a read-back to what the child said and blocks one that moves their view towards a position (P5); whether summaries are faithful enough is OA-56.

The cost is stated in D44 itself: at launch the Primer will say "not yet" often, and how often is Unknown until children use it (OA-32). That cost is accepted because the alternative is a product whose most important property, that it can be trusted with a child's questions, rests on nothing. The films raise it: an entry or pack goes live only with its reviewed explainer, so one still waiting for its drawing is "not yet".

The demonstration answers any question, labels everything "not checked" and gives no sources (D71); none of that is the product's (P2).

The explanation bank

The explanation bank answers the factual part of the questions children actually ask: about nature and the body, the sky and the sea, machines, history, words and numbers, and now people's lives and work, with the figures and stories a choice needs (figures). It is not a syllabus and it is not exam content; that is the Tutor's item bank.

Each entry holds:

  • The questions it answers, in the several ways children phrase them.
  • An explanation written for an age band, in short plain sentences, spoken as well as shown. Where one explanation cannot serve the whole of 8 to 13, there are versions for the code's age ranges within it, Assumed (CV-06) (P4).
  • What is not known, or where scientists disagree, said as plainly as what is known. A child should learn early that "we don't know yet" is an answer adults give.
  • Its figures, each with its source, and marked where the source estimates.
  • The harder bit: the question underneath, which the Primer turns to with those words, pointing at the inquiry pack that takes it up. Every entry has one, since every answer takes the whole shape (D110); until its pack is reviewed, the question gets "not yet" and joins the writing queue.
  • A drawn explainer, specified in advance and reviewed with the text (D111, D73; explainers). What one costs to make and review is Unknown.
  • Provenance: the writer, the reviewer, the reference works consulted, the date and the version; for a figure, the date by which it is checked again; and each real person it names, so that every entry naming one person can be pulled at once (P5).

Entries carry identifiers of the form EB-SCI-SLEEP-002. Under each answer a small grey line, "Based on: ...", names the reference works the entry drew on (D109), so that a parent can look the source up, as the Tutor's transcripts show the item-bank node behind each statement (01); the identifier stays in the entry's provenance, for reviewers.

When a child asks something the bank cannot answer, the question is separated from the child's account and rewritten in general terms, with names, places and anything about the child's own life removed, then counted and added to a writing queue ordered by how often children ask it. The queue never holds what a child typed or said, or any link back to the child: no recording and no transcript, since a spoken question's audio is thrown away at once (D102, ahead of OI-17). Who or what does the rewriting, and how it is checked, is set with the safeguarding policy (P4). A question leaves the queue when the catalogue answers it or the review group retires it. The queue is the content roadmap: the catalogue grows towards what children want to know, not towards what writers find interesting. The rate at which it can grow is set by reviewers, not writers, and is Unknown until PM2 times it.

Below the grounding floor, the affected module stops composing factual answers and serves the entry text verbatim or "not yet" until the cause is found. The floor is the Tutor's P2 floor, 99.5% of sampled turns, adopted as a design choice rather than derived. D21 carries over with it: grounding is reported per session, not per turn, and no claim is made that the Primer "does not make things up".

Figures, odds and named people

The explanation bank holds the evidence a choice needs: careers data, odds and the documented stories of named people, living ones included. A living person is described only by public, documented facts from reviewed sources, never their private life, never quoted beyond the source and never voiced. A figure whose source is itself an estimate is said with "roughly" and keeps its source; a figure no reviewed entry holds is never said, so D44 is unchanged. Evidence follows the child's market: UK figures and cases for a child in the UK, US ones in the US.

Decision D113from the films, confirmed by the owner, 31 October 2026

A choice in a child's own future needs evidence, and this is how the bank holds it without saying anything no reviewer has checked:

  • A figure keeps its source: the work, its date and the group it counts, so that a figure about boys in England is said as one. A figure about most people is said as that, never as this child's chances (P4).
  • "Roughly", where the source estimates. The fact-checker marks each figure as a count or an estimate; an estimate is written and spoken with "roughly", and a turn that drops it is not entailed by its entry, so it fails T-PRM-01.
  • Figures that count different groups are never set side by side as if they counted the same one.
  • Figures go stale. An entry holding one carries a date for checking it again, after which the figure is not said until it has been.
  • The child's market, which is the account's, decides the evidence. An entry with national data or cases is written for each market, and a child whose market has none gets "not yet", never another country's figure.
  • A named person appears only through public, documented facts from reviewed sources: nothing private, no quotation beyond the source, never their voice, and the Primer never speaks as them (D47). A story is told as what happened, never as a model or a forecast for the child. What the law requires of such stories (privacy, defamation, rights in a name or image, the Children's code) is OI-30, which counsel has not answered (P4).

The football film's odds show why the rules are needed. They come from three sources that count different groups: the publisher's description of Michael Calvin's No Hunger in Paradise, that only 180 of the 1.5 million boys who play organised youth football in England will become Premier League professionals, Assumed (CV-31); Sky Sports News's own analysis, that 91 per cent of academy footballers never play a single game as a professional, Assumed (CV-32); and the PFA's statement that over 99 per cent of those who sign for an academy aged 9 do not make it as professional footballers, Assumed (CV-33). The register treats the first and third as estimates, and the second gives no cohort or method, so an entry holding them would say "roughly", name each source and keep the three apart. They are the film's content, registered so that this page can say what the film shows; whether the bank uses them is for its reviewers. No US figure is registered, so what a child in the US would hear is Unknown.

The film also tells the stories of Pike, Rooney and Vardy. This page says how the bank would hold stories like theirs, and states nothing about the three players.

The inquiry packs

An inquiry pack breaks one question people have argued about, such as "can you know you are not dreaming?", into its three simplest positions, each from a named thinker or tradition and each at its strongest, and holds every move the Primer makes with them, in the shape the films show:

Every answer has one shape: a checked fact with its source; "the harder bit"; the question broken into three simplest positions, each from a named thinker or tradition and each given its strongest case; "Which is closest to what you think?"; the other two ask back, as positions and never as people ("A Stoic would ask..."); the child's real question; probing with questions and evidence; the child's own position, with a reason, read back. Positions come from more than one culture across each module, not inside every question. D47 stands: the Primer never speaks as any real person.

Decision D110from the films, confirmed by the owner, 31 October 2026

A pack holds:

  • The question, phrased for each age band, and the bank entry whose checked fact opens it.
  • Three positions, each with the thinker or tradition named, a plain paraphrase, the strongest reason given for it, and where the writer found it.
  • Ask-backs: the question each position would put to each of the others, written as a position and never as a person (D47). Once the child picks the closest, the two not chosen ask back.
  • Moves to the real question, as when, in the football film, a question about school becomes one about the child's own future. If what comes out is about family, the body, feelings or safety, triage stops it (P5).
  • Probing: counterexamples and thought experiments that cut every way, and links back to the bank entries whose figures and stories the probing may use.
  • Moves for "Keep going": a deeper counterexample, a next question or a related pack, within the time cap, which cannot be extended (D103).
  • A contested flag. Most pack questions are contested. The flag switches on the check that the Primer never states a conclusion as its own (T-PRM-03).

Everything an answer says, from the opening fact to the read-back, is written to be spoken briskly, so that one question fits D112's design target of three to six minutes; how long each element may be is Unknown until PM3's pilot times the first modules.

Positions are attributed, never asserted. The Primer reports that Descartes argued something; it never adds that he was right. On a contested question every pack carries three positions, each at its strongest, and the balance reviewer checks that none is framed more favourably than the others. A third is never invented to fill the shape: a question with only two serious answers waits as "not yet", and how often that happens is part of what OA-58 tests.

That three positions, each at its strongest, can be written and reviewed for most questions children ask.

AssumedOA-58, tested at PM2

The packs draw on several traditions, deliberately, across each module rather than inside every question (D110). One question's three positions may come from one tradition; the module that holds it may not. A Primer that drew only on European philosophy would be teaching a child, without saying so, whose thinking counts. The range intended looks like this:

Chinese thought, such as Zhuangzi's butterfly dream, or Mencius and Xunzi disagreeing about whether people are good by nature; Indian philosophy, such as the Nyaya school's account of the ways we come to know things; Islamic philosophy, such as Ibn Sina's "floating man" thought experiment about the self; the Southern African idea of ubuntu, that a person is a person through other people; ancient Greek and modern European philosophy; and philosophy written for children today. These are examples to show the range intended, not content: each attribution is a writer's to make and a reviewer's to check.

Assumedillustrative, not yet written or reviewed

The football film's hedge is a position, not advice. The film ends its thread on "bet hard on what you believe in", and spread your bets, as fund managers do. Said to a child as a rule, that tells them what to do, which D106 forbids, so here the Primer departs from the film (D101): a way of reasoning like the hedge is one position in a pack, attributed to the practice it comes from, set beside positions that disagree with it, and never said as what this child should do (T-PRM-03). Betting stays a metaphor: no reply names gambling, a bookmaker or a stake of money (P4), and the module's reviewers check its betting words for the youngest band.

Drawn explainers

Each answer is drawn as it is spoken: an explainer that builds on the screen in time with the voice. It is made in advance as part of a reviewed explanation, as D73 requires, and drawn by code from its reviewed specification, so that a child sees the same drawing every time and nothing is generated live by an image or video model.

Decision D111from the films, confirmed by the owner, 31 October 2026

Every drawing is content, and its specification holds:

  • Steps, each keyed to a reviewed sentence of its entry or pack and drawn when the voice reaches that sentence, which is spoken word for word; a sentence the generator composes, a question or the read-back draws nothing. Each age version of an entry has its own steps.
  • Only what the entry holds. Every figure, name and label comes from its entry or pack, which a script checks before review, so a number the entry does not hold can no more be drawn than said.
  • Columns drawn alike on a contested pack, in an order the balance reviewer has checked.
  • No likeness. No photograph, crest or logo, and no likeness of a real person: a named person appears as a plain figure with a name, and whether even the name may be shown is part of OI-30.
  • Its own provenance and live flag, re-read at every turn (P5). An answer is served with its drawing or not at all, so pulling an explainer takes its answer out of service, and the child hears "not yet", until a corrected specification is reviewed.

An explainer designer writes the specification in the vocabulary the code can draw, and the reviewers of its entry or pack check it (review). At every release T-PRM-11 checks that what is drawn matches the specification, that every explained stage consists of placed reviewed sentences, that every reviewed sentence a turn places matches its entry word for word, and that the text sent to the voice is the text shown; the voice's output is checked by sampling, transcribed and compared with the text, when the voice is chosen at PM4 and at every voice update. How it is rendered is in P5. Nothing goes live without its explainer.

Two limits are real. Code draws only what its vocabulary holds, D28's cost for the Tutor's figures (06): a picture outside it needs engineering first, and the football film's pictures (dots that thin out to show the odds, three columns, a staircase, a grid of players, a stack) are its first test. And a drawing exists only for a question a module anticipated: the film's drawings follow the child's own question, and a drawing built from a child's own words cannot be made in advance, so the product never draws one.

That a drawn explainer can be specified and reviewed at the rate the catalogue needs.

AssumedOA-59, measured at PM2

The demonstration generates an eight-second clip live for each of its three building blocks, unchecked (D74, D76). Those rules are its alone: the product's explainers show words, numbers and people, under the rules above (P2).

Modules, which are sensitive, and choices ahead

The Primer's subjects come in modules the company writes and reviews. Parents switch modules on and off; modules on sensitive subjects start switched off; each module is written for an age band and offered only within it. Parents cannot add content or views.

DecisionD46, 22 October 2026

A module groups related packs and explanation-bank entries under a theme, with a description and example questions a parent reads before deciding: the topics that, in the sleep film, parents choose to switch on. The age band and the module switches are settings the verified adult makes, read at every session; they are not memory of the child (D117). They apply before anything is matched, so a module switched off or outside the band contributes nothing to any turn (T-PRM-05).

A subject is sensitive where families' beliefs differ, or where a child's own life is likely to come up: morality, death, religion and belief, politics and power. Sensitive modules start switched off and are written for 10 and over (D57); switching one on does not loosen anything else, so the contested-question check and triage apply inside it exactly as outside.

Choices in a child's own future are outside those rules by the owner's decision, and on them the Primer neither encourages nor discourages: it says what the reality is, and what the schools of thought say.

The Primer may reason with a child about a choice in their own future, such as whether to stake everything on football, weighing the odds and the evidence, ending on the child's own position and never telling them what to do. It is offered at every age from 8 to 13, and is on unless a parent turns it off. Questions about family, the body, feelings or safety still stop, and the child is pointed to a trusted adult. Asked how far it goes, the owner added on 31 October 2026: the questions about a child's future are not discouraging nor encouraging; the Primer merely acknowledges the reality and what various philosophies and schools of thought say about the topic.

Decision D106the owner's, 31 October 2026

The module that holds these questions, choices ahead, is offered at every age and is on unless a parent turns it off; without D106 it would start switched off and never reach a child of 8 or 9. It sets out the reality, from reviewed evidence with its source (figures), and what the schools of thought say about the choice, each at its strongest (packs); then it asks where the child stands and reads their position back. Its writers write nothing that tells a child what to do, predicts what will happen to them, judges their chances from what they say about themselves, or praises, warns or reassures them. Where the evidence describes what happens to most people, the entry says so, as the reality and not a verdict on the child. A question about family, the body, feelings or safety stops wherever it arises, even part way through a choice. The fence and its boundary cases are in P2 and P4; triage applies it on every turn (P5), and T-PRM-06 tests it.

That parents accept the module on by default is OA-57. Whether it engages duties beyond OI-16's is OI-32, which counsel has not answered. RP9 is the risk that it is reported as a product telling children what to do with their lives.

"Minds and machines" needs one rule of its own. Who writes, and who reviews

The writer and the reviewer are never the same person, the rule the Tutor applies to its item bank (03). Writers are philosophy educators, including people trained in Philosophy for Children, primary and lower-secondary teachers, and subject specialists for the explanation bank; the films add statistics specialists for figures, and explainer designers, who specify each drawing in the vocabulary the code draws from. Fact-checkers review every figure and every story of a named person.

Every reviewer also checks the manner rules, whatever the content: no "I", no child's name and no praise (D108).

The standing review group is the one page 13 requires: child-safety and education expertise, with the power to block a release, inspect red-team results and require a rollback (13). Because the owner wants the UK and the US at once (D51), review includes whether a module reads acceptably to families across the range of beliefs in both countries, and whether one catalogue can serve both is an assumption, OA-27. For figures and cases the catalogue is already two, since evidence follows the child's market (D113).

Provenance is recorded per entry, every change is reviewed again, and a figure is checked again by its date, because careers data and odds go stale while an entry stays the same. This is a rate-limited human process that does not get cheaper when the next model ships, which makes it the Primer's equivalent of the Tutor's examiner supply (12, R7), and the films enlarge it: a drawing for every answer, figures to check again, two markets' evidence. Unknown the cost of a module and the number of modules a review group can clear; PM2 measures both, with the time to specify and review explainers (OA-59), and RP5 is the risk that they are worse than the plan needs.

What may not enter

  • Reproduced copyrighted text. Philosophers are paraphrased with attribution. A modern translation is quoted only where the review group has confirmed it may be. Facts and figures from published works may be used with their source, their text paraphrased.
  • Commercial programme materials. Published Philosophy for Children session plans and curricula do not enter. A writer trained in those methods writes original material, and declares per pack that no programme material came with them, as the Tutor's validators do (D20).
  • Anything a parent writes. Parents send ideas and requests to review; nothing they write is content (D46).
  • A child's words. A question from the writing queue is rewritten, never copied, and the catalogue never contains anything a child typed or said: no recording and no transcript (D102, ahead of OI-17).
  • Pictures of real people and things. No photograph, crest or logo, and no likeness of a real person, enters an explainer. A named person appears only by name, and what the law requires even of that is OI-30, which counsel has not answered.
  • Anything about a living person beyond the public record. Nothing from their private life, none of their words beyond what the source quotes, and never their voice (D113, D47).
  • Anything without two names on it. No entry, pack, explainer or module goes live without a named writer and a different named reviewer.

What the evidence says about the method

The honest summary is that the nearest human method was evaluated in the EEF's two trials, a smaller one with small positive effects and a larger one that found no evidence of an effect on attainment Assumed (CV-05), and nothing in this vault shows that an AI-led version of it improves how children reason.

The Education Endowment Foundation evaluated Philosophy for Children twice. An efficacy trial reported in 2015, in Years 4 and 5, found small positive effects on reading and maths, at moderate security. A later and larger effectiveness trial, in Year 6, found no evidence of an effect on reading or maths, at the foundation's highest security rating, and the programme was removed from its list of promising projects. Teachers in the larger trial reported gains in pupils' respect for others' views that the pupil survey did not detect.

AssumedCV-05, awaiting the owner's check

Three consequences, all of them constraints on how the Primer is described.

  1. No attainment claim. The Primer is not sold as raising reading or maths scores. The more secure of the EEF's two trials found no evidence of such an effect for the human version Assumed (CV-05), and nothing suggests an AI-led version would do better.
  2. The outcome is reasoning itself, measured directly. If the Primer does anything, it should show in how a child gives reasons, weighs another view and responds to a counterexample. That is what T-PRM-07 measures, and the teachers' reports in the larger trial Assumed (CV-05) suggest it is at least the right thing to look for.
  3. Neither result may transfer. An AI-led dialogue with one child differs from a teacher-led discussion with a class in almost every respect that might matter: no classmates to disagree with, no teacher reading the room, and a conversation that can follow one child's question all the way down. The design's stand-in for classmates is the ask-back, in which the two positions the child did not choose put their questions to the one they did; whether it does what classmates do is untested. The positive result may not carry over, and neither may the null one (OA-25).

The evidence for AI tutoring that the Tutor relies on (00) is evidence about AI supporting the teaching of a subject. It says nothing about a child's reasoning, and the Primer does not borrow it.

The films are no evidence either. They are illustrative and made with AI: they show what the design would look like, not a working system, and nothing in them shows that it helps a child.

How we would know it works

The Primer works if children who use it reason better, unaided, than comparable children who do not. Minutes, sessions and return visits are not outcomes; page 13 rules them out as success measures (13), and D25 rules out optimising for them. Nor is how often a child presses "Keep going": nothing is tuned to raise it, and the close offers its two choices with equal weight (D103, D25).

T-PRM-07 is designed like the Tutor's unaided check (D15): the measurement happens without the Primer. Children answer a short set of open questions, before and after a block of sessions, and raters who do not know which group a child is in, or whether an answer came before or after, score them against a rubric. Because children may answer the Primer aloud, and writing at 8 or 9 limits what can show, the evaluator settles at PM4 whether answers are written, spoken and transcribed, or both; spoken audio is thrown away once transcribed. Whether answers may be spoken, and how any audio is handled, is set in PM4's research protocol and its DPIA, with OI-17 open (P4). The rubric:

  • gives a reason for a view;
  • considers another view, and states it fairly;
  • responds to a counterexample rather than ignoring it;
  • separates what they know from what they believe;
  • changes their mind for a reason, and says why.

That a change in how a child reasons can be measured this way, sensitively enough to show a difference in a pilot. Unknown the effect size worth detecting, the number of children that would need, and how well raters agree; all three are set with the independent evaluator at PM4.
