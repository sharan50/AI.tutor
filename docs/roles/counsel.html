Counsel

You own the rights dossier, the safety, privacy and regulatory chapter, the contracts, the counsel questions in the open-items register, and the safety documents of the Primer and of Foundations, whose users are almost all under thirteen and which raise questions of their own, as well as the safety and privacy of the Primer's demonstration and the Tutor's MVP, which only adults use. A launch in the United States, which all three ideas now plan alongside the UK where resources allow, adds questions under US law. Nothing has been built. The questions are deliberately unanswered and stay open until you answer them; the two exceptions, naming the board and the self-tutor, are recorded as decisions taken ahead of your advice with their containment written down, and each says so wherever it is applied. You read the data and consent sections and the compliance sections of both routes, because they apply your positions rather than set them. Where a page says a marker is Assumed, the reasoning is visible and could be wrong; where it says Open, the vault is waiting on you.

Decisions

  • D13 No offshore litigation-evasion strategy. The Indian company is retained for ordinary tax and capital reasons.
  • D24 Zero retention of prompts and outputs, and no training on our data, as a model vendor procurement gate.
  • D25 No engagement objective anywhere in the system. No streaks, badges, loss aversion or return notifications.
  • D26 A safeguarding disclosure is not automatically reported to the parent.
  • D34 Name the board in public copy, under a fixed convention, ahead of counsel's answer on OI-3.
  • D35 Ship a provisional grade range, and gate nothing on it.
  • D46 The Primer's subjects come in modules the company writes and reviews. Parents switch modules on and off; modules on sensitive subjects start switched off; each module is written for an age band and offered only within it. Parents cannot add content or views; they may send ideas and requests, which go to the company's review and are never shown to anyone else.
  • D48 A parent sees an activity record of their child's Primer use: modules used, questions asked and the views the child recorded, except that a question the Primer declined, or one in a switched-off module, appears by its category, not its words. For a child under 13 the full conversation is also available to the parent on request. An exchange flagged for safeguarding, the Primer's own reply included, is withheld from the record and from any transcript and routed to a trained reviewer, as D26 requires. Taken while OI-8 and OI-14 are open; it answers neither.
  • D49 The Primer remembers ideas, not lives: the questions a child asked, the views they recorded and the modules they used, and nothing about their family, friends or feelings beyond the session.
  • D50 Neither the Primer nor Foundations collects a child's voice or image at launch.
  • D56 The Foundations record holds what the adult tapped and nothing else: no free-text notes about the child, and no inferred difficulty, need or condition.
  • D57 Within its band, the Primer's settings follow the Children's code's age ranges: 8 and 9, 10 to 12, and 13. Modules on sensitive subjects are written for 10 and over, so a child of 8 or 9 is offered none. The range is set from the child's age in years as the verified adult gives it, never a date of birth.
  • D60 The self-tutor is the learner's alone. It plays only in the learner's own session, behind a passcode the learner sets when it is made; no surface the product gives a parent, school, creator or anyone else shows or plays it; nothing it renders can be shared, exported or downloaded; and the parent report shows the same progress for self-tutor sessions as for any other, never the likeness.
  • D62 Before capture, the verified adult and the learner each consent, separately and explicitly, after being told in plain words what is kept, that the product shows it to no one else, and how to delete it. Either can withdraw at any time, which deletes the self-tutor. It is off by default.
  • D77 Safety in the demo: a safety check runs before anything is shown; a message about the tester's own life, a death or serious illness close to them, their safety or a big subject such as religion or sex gets a reply written in advance, and nothing about it reaches the video model; the reply about safety names Childline and, for an adult, Samaritans in the UK, and Childhelp and the 988 lifeline in the US; the welcome page says adults only; a passcode protects the site and every paid call; and spending is capped at each provider and in the app.
  • D79 The demo keeps no record of who asked what: no accounts, no recordings and no conversation logs. It keeps the generated material in its cache, by concept; each day's short answers, to read them aloud, until the day ends; and timings and counts without content, so that the owner can measure the waits and the spend.
  • D81 Only adults use the MVP, playing learners of 14 to 18. No child uses it, and it holds no child's data.
  • D87 Testers' data: accounts by invitation; transcripts and progress kept for the test period and deleted at its end, with everything else about testers except counts and timings; likenesses under D63's rules; and nothing about a tester's life beyond what they type.

Key blocks

Read plainly, the second limb bars an AI tutoring product from containing AQA material at all, not merely from training on it. That is a wider prohibition than the AI-training clauses most publishers have adopted, and it is the reason the clean-room policy is absolute rather than a training-time restriction.

That line is the whole content strategy, so it is written as an operational rule rather than as a principle. The rule lives in 03 and is enforced by the provenance record on every item, the two-person rule, the per-item attestation, and the validator engagement terms that forbid a current examiner bringing board material into the work.

There is no statutory personality right in the UK. Protection of a creator's persona runs through: passing off for false endorsement (following Irvine v Talksport), trade marks, copyright in the underlying videos, performers' rights, and data protection insofar as a voice or image is personal data. This framing has not been confirmed by counsel for this fact pattern.

Assumptionnot verified, counsel's likely framing

One page, because there is almost nothing in it. The creator licenses a name and a face to label a teaching style. They license nothing they have made.

Fifteen minutes to disabled, seven days to deleted, thirty-five days to gone from backups. Measured from receipt of a valid notice at a published address, not from when someone reads it.

Because of D8. No persona LoRAs, no fine-tuned weights, no adapters carrying creator style, ever. Retrieval and prompting only. Weights cannot be cleanly revoked, and a kill switch that requires retraining is not a kill switch; it is a project plan. The fifteen-minute figure is only credible because there is nothing to retrain, and any future proposal to fine-tune on persona data should be read as a proposal to delete this table.

When the face and voice are the learner's own, the persona problem this dossier is built around mostly disappears, and a different one takes its place. For an archetype in the learner's own likeness, nobody else's identity is used, so in the vault's reading there is no endorsement to imply and no licence to take; whether that holds is Open OI-27. A named preset is different, and needs Schedule 4. The risk moves to misuse: a self-tutor made of someone who is not the learner.

This runs the opposite way to how it is usually read. Building in India does not put the venture further from a creator's reach; on personality rights it puts it closer, because Indian personality-rights jurisprudence is currently the most aggressive in the world on AI likeness.

The courts are the slow, unlikely path. Payment processors and app stores are the fast, likely one, and they decide on a complaint rather than on a judgment. Plan for the chokepoint, not for the trial.

The conclusion a lawyer should take from that table, and it is not the one a founder expects: the legal merits of our position have almost no bearing on the events most likely to stop us. An app store does not weigh the clean-room policy. A processor does not read the licence. What they respond to is a complaint and a risk assessment, and what answers them is a pre-assembled evidence pack and a second supplier. Both are cheap and both are listed at M1 in 11 Roadmap.

Decision D13. The Indian operating company is retained for ordinary tax and capital reasons. The "fight it in India" rationale is dropped, because the theory runs the opposite way to how it was first read.

D11 is a hard requirement, and this is where the proof of it belongs, because it is a rights property before it is a product property. The claim is: no single unsigned contract can block launch, and no signed contract ending can stop the service.

Point 5 is the difference between proving and asserting. Without a blocking test that runs with an empty registry, D11 degrades into a claim that was true on the day it was written, and the first persona-dependent code path added under delivery pressure would falsify it silently.

Our entire user base is children, which inverts the usual problem. We are not trying to keep children out of an adult service; we are running a service in which every single user is a child, and therefore the Children's code is not an edge case to handle but the specification we build to. The risk is not that a child gets in. It is that we process a child's data without a basis we can defend, profile them in a way we cannot justify, or build something that keeps them on the app.

There is no date-of-birth field anywhere in the product. A child is never asked their age, because a child's answer would be the weakest possible signal and asking for it would create a record we would then have to justify holding.

Cost per verified adult. Not known, not in the verified findings, and not invented here. It is a one-off charge against the contribution of a whole acquisition, not of a month: 10 shows it subtracting directly from (p - c) x m over the examination cycle. If it consumes a large share of that, Route A's economics fail at the front door and Route B becomes the default. That is written as an explicit condition on the recommendation in 09 rather than left as a footnote.

Unknownrequired input, and it can break Route A on its own

No model vendor is used unless the contract provides zero retention of prompts and outputs, and no use of our data for training. This is not a preference. The marketing line below says "we do not use it to train anyone's AI model", and that clause is only true if the vendor contract makes it true. A vendor that cannot offer it is disqualified regardless of model quality, and the term is checked at procurement and at every renewal.

Decision D2414 September 2026, and it is a procurement gate

On request: inaccessible in the product immediately, purged from primary stores within 7 days, aged out of backups within 35 days. The backup figure is stated on the marketing site rather than hidden, because a deletion promise with a silent exception is the kind of thing an ICO audit finds.

Why Article 22 is not engaged. Article 22 bites on a decision, based solely on automated processing, which produces legal effects concerning the data subject or similarly significantly affects them. The design does not reach the first element, there being no decision; and even if it did, it would not reach the last, there being no significant effect. The middle point below is not an element of the test but the design constraint that keeps the first one true.

At launch, CINE is not a user-to-user service. There is no path by which any user encounters content generated by, uploaded by, or attributable to another user. Sessions are one to one with the tutor. Style adjustments are private to the learner who made them. There is no messaging, no sharing, no gallery, no group session and no user-created persona.

Since 23 October 2026, the self-tutor (D59) is a persona made from a learner's own face and voice. It is private to that learner (D60), so no user encounters another's, and the decision above stands in the vault's reading; but the self-tutor makes the question arguable, which is OI-24. The trigger list holds that nothing which makes the question arguable ships without an Online Safety Act duties assessment signed off before the work starts, so the self-tutor needs one.

The decisionwritten down, 14 September 2026

That a private one-to-one tutor with no sharing between users sits outside the OSA's user-to-user duties. This is reasoned from Ofcom's published examples and needs counsel. It is open item OI-4. The decision above is recorded as our position with its basis, which is what a regulator asks for; it is not a conclusion that the question is settled.

Assumptionnot verified, and this is the one to take to counsel first

Any one of the following converts the service into a user-to-user service, or makes the question genuinely arguable. None of them ships without a completed Online Safety Act duties assessment signed off before the work starts, and this list is a change-control gate in the engineering process, not a note in a design document.

#Feature that flips the analysis
T1Any way for a user to share tutor-generated content with another user inside the service.
T2Any way for a user to create, name, customise or publish a persona that another user can access. This is the trigger Ofcom named directly.
T3Group or class sessions in which learners can see each other's contributions.
T4A gallery, feed, leaderboard or showcase that surfaces one user's work or identity to another.
T5Messaging, comments, or any direct channel between users, including between a learner and a tutor who is a user.
T6A referral or social mechanic that surfaces one user's content or identity to another.
T7Search across other users' material.
T8Any third-party integration that republishes session content to a platform where other users see it.
T9Any way for anyone but the learner to see or hear a learner's self-tutor, or to obtain anything it renders.

The self-tutor is the one place the product holds a child's face and voice. It is off by default, made only with two consents, shown and played by the product to no one but the learner, and switched off at the next turn when either consent is withdrawn. Whether that is enough is a set of questions for counsel that this document does not answer.

  1. Acknowledge briefly and without alarm. One sentence. Not a wall of text, not a clinical script.
  2. Signpost to a named service that is actually staffed. In the UK: Childline on 0800 1111, and Samaritans on 116 123. Named, specific, and immediate, because these are available 24 hours and we are not.
  3. Escalate, and say so. The session is flagged to the named safeguarding role. The learner is told that a person will look at it, because telling a child you are passing something on is part of doing it properly.

A safeguarding disclosure is not automatically reported to the parent. Automatic parental notification is unsafe in precisely the cases that matter most, because the parent is sometimes the risk. Disclosures route to a trained human reviewer who decides, against a written policy, whether and whom to inform.
