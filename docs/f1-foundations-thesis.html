Foundations: thesis

Foundations helps an adult teach a child of four to seven to read, write and count. It is the least certain of the three ideas in this vault, its owner is sceptical of it, and it does not go first. What survives that scepticism is a narrow idea, and more modest about what a screen can do: a screen that coaches the adult, while the adult does the teaching only a person can do.

F2 Foundations: product

Foundations Ages 4 to 7

A coach for the adult teaching a young child to read, write and count.

A screen cannot guide a five-year-old's finger along a line of print, form a letter with their hand, or tell when they have had enough. A parent or teaching assistant can. Foundations keeps that adult at the centre of every session and gives them a teacher's know-how: the next step in a planned sequence, practice words using only sounds the child knows, what to listen for, and when to stop. It never listens to the child, never watches them and never rewards them for staying on.

If anyone pays, it is parents or schools, less urgently than for exams: the owner's view, still untested.

Designed, not built. The least certain of the three ideas, and deliberately not the first.

The claim, in four sentences

Foundations helps an adult teach a child of four to seven to read, write and count. The adult does the teaching that needs a person: a finger moving along the print, a hand forming a letter, real objects to count, and the judgement to stop at the right moment. The screen supplies what a trained early-years teacher carries in their head and an adult without that training does not: the next step in a planned sequence, practice built only from what the child has already been taught, what to listen for, and what to say when the child gets it wrong. It is the least certain of the three ideas, and it does not go first.

The age band is four to seven, set with the owner alongside the Primer's and the Tutor's. Above it the Primer begins, and a child who reads independently has outgrown Foundations, which is the point of it.

Decision D4122 October 2026

The product's user is the adult, not the child. That single choice, recorded as D43, decides almost everything else in these six documents: there is no conversation between the product and the child, no microphone, no camera, no reward loop, and no session without a person in the room. It is also the choice that makes Foundations the most limited of the three ideas, because every session costs an adult's time.

The owner's caution, stated plainly

This idea is in the vault because the owner wants it recorded and thought about properly, not because the owner is persuaded by it. The caution is the owner's and it is set out here in substance, without softening, because it is the starting point for the design rather than an objection to be argued down.

A five-year-old will not sit still to be taught by a screen. At this age, learning to read and write is physical. A person points along the line with the child's finger, forms letters with them, and builds a link between hand and mind that a screen cannot. A person also manages the child: notices the fidgeting, changes the activity, calls it a day. That is half of teaching a child this young, and it is why children of this age are taught by people.

The owner's viewOA-28, testable by asking teachers

Schools already teach this for free, so the routes to revenue are fewer than for the Tutor, whose families are already paying for exam tutoring. And the risk that it does not work is the highest of the three ideas.

The owner's viewOA-29, testable by asking parents

Foundations does not go first. The three reasons above are the reasons. Between the Tutor and the Primer, which goes first is open and is decided by field research.

Decision D4222 October 2026

Two of the three reasons are assumptions that a few weeks of conversations can test: whether children of this age learn early reading poorly from a screen without an adult (OA-28), and whether their parents feel less urgency to pay (OA-29). Both are flagged as cheap in the register and both have questions written for them in the field research guide, including questions designed to prove the owner wrong. The third reason, the size of the risk, is a judgement, and it follows from the first two.

The novel that gave the idea of a Primer its name raises the same question. In The Diamond Age, the book that teaches a girl to read and think is voiced live by a human actor who becomes something like a mother to her, and the novel leaves open how much of what she becomes came from the machine and how much from the person. Assumed CV-04, from summaries of the novel; fiction, cited for the question it raises and not as evidence.

What YC asked for, and why the owner disagrees on the order

Y Combinator's request for a Primer asks for adaptive teaching of reading, writing and arithmetic to young children, at close to the quality of a private tutor and at consumer scale, supplementing teachers rather than replacing them, and starting as something parents buy for basic skills. It names as the long-term goal the Primer of The Diamond Age, which teaches a girl not only to read but to think and reason. Secondary write-ups give the ages as four to ten.

AssumedCV-01, awaiting the owner's check; the primary page could not be opened from the build environment

Of the three ideas, Foundations is the closest to YC's request as it is reported, and the request's order puts it first: basic skills bought by parents are the way in, and the thinking Primer is the long-term goal Assumed CV-01. The owner disagrees with that order, for the reasons above, and D42 records the disagreement. An application to YC therefore has to argue against the sequence YC's own request sets out, and should do so openly rather than hope it goes unnoticed.

The strongest case for starting with basic skills

It deserves stating, because a reviewer will make it. Basic skills are what parents of young children already worry about. Reading progress is easier to observe than progress in reasoning, so a product that improves it is easier to prove and to sell. A child who starts with a product at five might stay with it for years. And a thinking tutor for older children is harder to evaluate, harder to explain, and more exposed to criticism about influence, all of which argues for earning trust with something plainer first.

The owner's answer

The answer is not that basic skills matter less. It is that the medium is wrong for the age. A product that only works on a screen, aimed at a child who cannot yet sit still for one, fails the child it is for, however good its sequencing (OA-28). And it competes with a school that already teaches the same thing at no cost to the family (OA-29). The design in these documents takes a different position: Foundations supplements the adult who is already teaching the child, and the child never uses the screen alone (D43).

The honest condition: if OA-28 turns out to be wrong, and children of four to seven do learn to read well from a screen without an adult, then the order in YC's request Assumed CV-01 deserves a second look and D42 should be revisited with a written reason. That is a result the field research and FM3 can produce, which is why it is stated here rather than defended against.

Why the adult is the point

Foundations is designed for an adult beside the child in every session. The adult is the primary user; the child is the learner. A child-alone mode is not a later feature, and the design does not leave room for one.

Decision D4322 October 2026

The adult supplies four things a screen cannot, on the owner's view of how children this age learn (OA-28):

  1. Attention. Noticing that a child has stopped listening, and changing what happens next. The product can suggest when to stop; only a person in the room can see that it is time.
  2. The physical side of reading and writing. A finger under each letter as the sounds are blended; a hand guided through the shape of a letter, in the air, in sand or on paper; real objects lined up and counted.
  3. Warmth. The child is learning with someone who cares whether they enjoy it, which is not something the product can supply and not something it pretends to.
  4. Judgement. Whether today is a day for reading at all.

The product supplies what a trained early-years teacher knows and an adult without that training does not: which sound comes next, which words a child can read with only the sounds they know, what a particular mistake means, and how to respond to it without discouraging them. The product is the adult's coach. The adult is the child's teacher.

This has a cost that should be faced in the first document rather than discovered in the last. Every session needs an adult with time, and the families with the least time are often the families whose children most need the help. A product that only works where an adult can sit for each session reproduces the inequality private tutoring already has. That is OA-30, it is not cheap to test, and it is risk RF1 in F6.

What the screen adds

The Tutor's thesis is that it sells a warranted answer rather than a clever one (00). Foundations sells a warranted next step. Everything it tells the adult is checked, and where a check can be a fixed rule rather than a model's judgement, it is.

What the screen gives the adultWhat makes it trustworthyWhere specified
The next step in a planned sequence of sounds and number ideas, one new thing per session.A sequence written originally to the published expectations English schools work to Assumed CV-09, and checked so that nothing is introduced out of order (T-FND-02).F3
Practice words and short texts built only from sounds the child has been taught.A deterministic decodability rule, with no model involved, that blocks any text containing a word the child cannot yet decode (T-FND-01).F5
What to listen for, and what to say after a particular mistake.Responses written and reviewed in advance by people who teach this age, not generated during the session.F3

When to stop.A short session by design, and a guide that says stop early and means it.F2
A record, so the next session starts in the right place.Built from what the adult taps, and nothing else.F2

The screen does not listen to the child, does not watch the child and does not talk to the child. The adult is the ears: they hear the child read and tap what happened. Listening to a child read is a possible later feature, separately consented, with no audio kept.

Decision D5022 October 2026

Nor does it reward the child. There are no stars, points, streaks or characters, for the reason D25 gives for the Tutor: a product with no engagement objective has no reason to build them, and the youngest users are the last people a reward loop should be pointed at. Praise in Foundations comes from the adult, and the guide suggests what to praise.

Where the case is weak

In order of how much each would matter if it went wrong.

  1. The design rests on the owner's view, not on evidence. OA-28 shapes everything here. If it is wrong, the design spends an adult's time on what a child could do alone. If it is right but unproven, it is an argument an investor can wave away. The evidence register holds nothing on how children of four to seven best learn to read, with software or without; F3 says so, and the cheapest remedy is reading the published research, not building.
  2. Nobody may pay. The register's tutoring figures describe secondary pupils (00) and say nothing about this age. What families spend on early reading help is Unknown. School teaches it for free, and OA-29 says parents of young children feel less urgency than parents of exam candidates.
  3. It needs an adult's time every session, which most limits the families who most need it (OA-30, RF1).
  4. Its sequence may clash with the school's. English schools may choose from a list of validated phonics programmes, and are not required to use one from it Assumed CV-09. That the programmes differ in the order and names of sounds is inferred, not checked; if they do, a home product that teaches sounds in a different order, or names them differently, can confuse a child in Reception. F3 proposes following the school's order where the adult can tell us what it is; whether adults can is untested.
  5. The category is not empty. The register holds no survey of the early-reading products already on sale. Until someone has read what they do and claim, Foundations' difference from them is asserted, not shown. That is a reading task at FM1.
  6. It has had one independent review and no adversarial rounds. These pages are a first draft, revised once after that review; the Tutor's documents needed eight rounds to find what was wrong with them.

None of these is hidden in an appendix, because the purpose of these documents is to let the owner decide, on evidence, whether Foundations is worth building at all. F6 states what evidence would justify it and what would end it.
