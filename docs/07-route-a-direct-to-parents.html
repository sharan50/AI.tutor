The June cliff, first

A Year 11 concentration means the customer's need ends on a date everyone knows in advance. That is not a churn problem to be reduced; it is the structure of the demand, and a plan that models it as reducible churn is lying to itself.

Year 11 is the peak tutoring year at 25% of pupils. Year 10 is next at 10%. The gap between them is the shape of the problem.

VerifiedSutton Trust, Private Tutoring 2026, February 2026

Three consequences, and the third is the one that matters to an investor.

  1. Retained months are structurally capped. A household acquired in January has at most five or six months of need. One acquired in September has nine. Nobody has thirty-six. Whatever the dominant variable in 10 turns out to be, it operates against a ceiling set by the examination calendar rather than by product quality.
  2. Revenue is seasonal and the seasonality is not smoothable. Reporting monthly recurring revenue for this business would flatter it from September to May and then expose it. Cohort contribution over an examination cycle is the correct unit of account, and it should be the unit used internally from the first customer, not adopted later when MRR becomes embarrassing.
  3. Acquisition has to refill from below, every year. The Year 10 figure of 10% is the ladder. A household that arrives in Year 10 has a plausible eighteen-month life; one that arrives in Year 11 has months. Acquisition cost per retained month is therefore radically different by year group, and the two should never be averaged together.

What is actually done about it

  • Price and sell the examination cycle, not the month. A cycle plan running to the last paper matches what the household is buying. Monthly billing invites a monthly cancellation decision at exactly the point revision gets unpleasant.
  • Acquire Year 10 in the autumn deliberately, accepting a worse conversion rate for a much better retained-month figure, and measure the two cohorts separately.
  • Do not fight the June exit. Reactivation of a Year 11 leaver is close to worthless; they have finished. Effort is better spent on the younger sibling, which is a household relationship rather than a learner relationship, and on the A-level continuation for the fraction who go on.
  • Do not use retention mechanics to soften it. D25 in 05 forbids streaks, badges and return notifications. The June cliff is the moment that decision costs the most, and it is precisely the moment it would be most tempting to quietly reverse.

Unit economics structure

The full model and the identification of the dominant variable are in 10. What is specific to Route A:

  • Age verification is a one-off cost at acquisition, charged against the contribution of the whole acquisition rather than of the first month, per the arithmetic in 10. Unknown Its size, per 05. It subtracts from the acquisition budget pound for pound.
  • Payment processing on a low monthly ticket is a material percentage, not a rounding error.
  • Variable session cost is bounded per turn by D30, so it scales with usage rather than with session length. A heavy user is expensive linearly, not quadratically.
  • CAC is entirely ours, unlike Route B where a school contract amortises across a cohort.

Data and consent model

We are the controller. Everything in 05 falls on us directly, with no school to share it with.

  • The adult is the account holder, age-verified before any learner profile exists. No date of birth is collected from anyone.
  • The child is the data subject for the tutoring data, while the contract is held by the parent. That mismatch is open item OI-10 and it is the sharpest privacy question in this route.
  • Parental visibility is objective-level, not transcript-level, by default (D16).
  • Safeguarding escalation cannot route to a school because there is not one, so it routes to our own reviewer, with the honest launch limitation stated in 05: next business day, which is not adequate for an acute disclosure.

That last point is the real compliance asymmetry between the routes, and it is worth more weight than it usually gets: in Route A we carry a safeguarding duty we are not staffed to discharge, and the mitigation is to signpost to services that are.

Distribution motion

The verified concentration data says where demand already is. It has to be read carefully, because one reading of it is unlawful.

London 45%, rest of England 27%, Wales 24%. Urban 33%, rural 19%. By ethnicity: Black pupils 64%, Asian 50%, White 20%; in deprived areas, 65%, 43% and 10% respectively. Worst-off households 23%, best-off 30%.

VerifiedSutton Trust, February 2026

The ethnicity figures inform where to test channels, by geography and school catchment, and inform nothing else. No advertising is targeted on a protected characteristic. Ethnicity is not collected (05), cannot be inferred, and is not an input to targeting, pricing or product.

The figures are a fact about where tutoring demand already exists. Turning them into an audience segment would be unlawful, and would also be the version of this venture nobody should want to build.

Decision D3114 September 2026

Channels, in order of expected efficiency

  1. Topic-level search intent. The unit of demand is "why does the sign flip", not "maths tutor". Content answering exactly those questions, at the objective granularity the item bank is already organised around, is the lowest-cost channel available and it is a by-product of building the bank.
  2. Geography. London and urban catchments, per the verified figures, without reference to any protected characteristic.
  3. The household, not the learner. A parent with one Year 11 often has a Year 8. The retained-month ceiling in the June cliff is a learner ceiling, not a household one.
  4. A signed creator's own audience. Potentially the cheapest channel of all, and the subject of the next section.

The tension between the wedge and D11

D11 says the product launches and runs with zero signed creators, and 04 proves that technically: no content depends on a persona, the archetype path is half the launch product, and T-ZERO-01 blocks any release that breaks it.

That proof is about the product, not the business. An educator with an audience of GCSE students is simultaneously the wedge and the cheapest acquisition channel available. If creator-led acquisition turns out to be the only affordable CAC, then the business depends on signed creators even though the product does not, and D11's independence is technically intact while being commercially hollow.

How this is kept honest: CAC is measured separately for creator-led and non-creator-led acquisition from the first cohort. If non-creator CAC is unaffordable, that is reported as a finding about D11's commercial reality rather than absorbed into a blended average that hides it. This is scheduled with the D14 instrumentation at M4 in 11 Roadmap.

What breaks first

In order of likelihood, with how it would be noticed.

  1. The price anchor is software, not tutoring. Noticed as a landing-page conversion rate that collapses above about £10 a month. Detectable before any product exists, which is why M5 comes early.
  2. Age verification cost exceeds first-month contribution. Noticed the moment a provider quotes. Detectable this quarter, by asking.
  3. Non-creator CAC is unaffordable and the business quietly becomes creator-dependent. Noticed only if CAC is split by channel from the first cohort, which is why the split is specified above rather than assumed.
  4. Retained months are shorter than the examination calendar suggests, because households buy in March rather than September. Noticed in the first cohort's contribution figures, and it is the variable 10 identifies as dominant.
  5. The refusal behaviour costs retention. A tutor that declines questions a free chatbot answers is a worse experience in the moment. Noticed as a correlation between gate-fire rate and session-two return rate, which is measurable from the first live week and is the reason the gate-fire rate is instrumented in 06.
