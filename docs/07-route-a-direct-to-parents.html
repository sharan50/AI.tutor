Route A: direct to parents

The owner's preferred route. A parent buys, a student uses, the company is an independent controller and carries the full weight of that.

Why this route has the evidence behind it

Tutoring uptake among secondary pupils in England and Wales is 29% and rising, peaking at 25% in Year 11 and 45% in London, while school-organised tutoring fell to 20% of pupils and 58% of schools cut their offer. The category is moving from institutions to families.

VerifiedSutton Trust, Private Tutoring 2026

Pricing

The reference point is not Netflix, it is the £25 to £45 an hour a parent currently pays for GCSE tutoring. A monthly subscription that replaces even one hour a month is defensible. The actual number cannot be set until the cost per assured account and the cost per session are known.

The seasonality problem

Demand peaks in Year 11 and collapses the week after the last exam. This is the defining economic feature of the route and it is not solvable by discounting.

Three partial answers, none tested: annual pricing bought in September rather than monthly bought in April; Year 10 as the acquisition year with Year 11 as the retention year; and a materially cheaper out-of-season tier that keeps the account alive rather than closing it.

Distribution

The creators are the channel and the product feature at once, which is the strongest structural argument for this route. A creator with a GCSE audience who is also a named preset has a reason to mention it that is not advertising.

This makes creator acquisition the single point of failure for Route A distribution, which sits uncomfortably with the requirement that the product run with zero licensed personas. Both are true and the tension is real: zero-persona operation protects the launch date, it does not protect the growth plan.
