Three ideas for teaching children with AI

Each is for a different age, and each rests on the same rule: what it says must be accountable. One prepares teenagers for their exams. One takes a child's why-questions seriously and asks what they think, and why. One tells the teacher of a young class which child needs help, and with what.

Nothing described here has been built yet. Each idea below has its own design, its evidence and an honest account of what is still unknown. Only one will go first, and that choice is still open.

The three ideas

The Tutor Ages 14 to 18

An exam tutor that answers for what it teaches, in the style the student chooses.

A GCSE or A-level student picks a named educator whose teaching they already like, and the Tutor teaches in that style, in their own face and voice if they choose. Everything it teaches comes from questions written to the exam specification and checked by examiners, and a statement it cannot trace to one of them is blocked before the student sees it. Free AI study tools reason better in general; the Tutor competes on being answerable for correctness where an exam is at stake.

Parents pay; the untested bet is that they will price it against a private tutor, not a free app.

The most developed of the three ideas: a full design and a recommended route to market. Nothing is built and no test has been run.

The Primer Ages 8 to 13

A tutor for a child's why-questions, designed to ask what they think rather than tell them.

A child asks why we sleep, or whether a robot can think, and the Primer takes the question seriously: a checked explanation first, then the bigger question underneath, explored the way a good tutor would, by asking rather than telling, until the child can say what they think and why. It draws on thinkers from many traditions and does not give its own view where people disagree. Parents choose which topics are switched on. Who pays is the open question: parents, with no exam to prepare for.

Designed, not built. Nothing here has been tried with a child yet.

Foundation Ages 4 to 7

A quiet instrument that tells the teacher which child needs help, and with what. Its aim, in the film's closing words: no one gets left behind.

A teacher with a whole class cannot see every page at once. Foundation is designed to. A small camera over each desk watches the page and the hand, never the face, and reads the sum, the sentence and the pencil grip as they happen. When a child goes wrong, that child's tile on the teacher's screen turns amber with what happened and a suggested next step. The child sees nothing, hears nothing and is never rewarded; the teacher is always the one who acts.

Sold to schools, as a tool for the teacher. Whether schools will pay for a unit on every desk is untested.

Designed, not built. Not the first of the three ideas, by the owner's order.

Which goes first

Foundation does not go first. That is the owner's order, and it is not a judgement that Foundation is the lesser idea. The reasons D42 gave described a design for a parent at home, which the owner has replaced with a classroom instrument for the teacher, and they are withdrawn with it. Between the Tutor and the Primer the choice is open. It will be made on what teachers, professors and parents say in the field research, against one rule written down in advance, and recorded in the decision ledger with what it rules out.

DecisionD94, the owner's, 29 October 2026; D42's order kept

What the three share

All three rest on one rule carried over from the Tutor: nothing reaches a child that cannot be traced to a source someone has checked, and what cannot be traced is blocked, or answered with an honest "not yet". All three refuse engagement tricks, with no streaks, badges or reminders; none acts as a friend; an adult is always accountable; and any piece of content can be withdrawn in one step. They share a platform, not a customer: the ages, the buyers, the data and the law all differ.

DecisionD40, D41, D44, D25

What happens next

Before anything is built, the owner will put each idea to the people who would have to believe in it: teachers who have taught these ages, professors who study how children learn and think, and parents who would pay. The questions are written to find out what would make each idea wrong, not to collect encouragement, and every answer is recorded against the assumption it tests.
