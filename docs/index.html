Three ideas for teaching children with AI

Each is for a different age, and each rests on the same rule: a tutor should be accountable for what it says. One prepares teenagers for their exams. One takes a child's why-questions seriously and asks what they think, and why. One helps an adult teach a young child to read, write and count.

Nothing described here has been built yet. Each idea below has its own design, its evidence and an honest account of what is still unknown. Only one will go first, and that choice is still open.

The three ideas

The Tutor Ages 14 to 18

An exam tutor that answers for what it teaches, in the style the student chooses.

A GCSE or A-level student picks a named educator whose teaching they already like, and the Tutor teaches in that style. Everything it teaches comes from questions written to the exam specification and checked by examiners, and a statement it cannot trace to one of them is blocked before the student sees it. The free AI study tools are better general reasoners; the Tutor competes on being answerable for correctness where an exam is at stake.

Parents pay, and the bet is that they will price it against a private tutor rather than a free app; that is still to be tested.

The most developed of the three ideas: a full design and a recommended route to market. Nothing is built and no test has been run.

The Primer Ages 8 to 13

A tutor for a child's why-questions, designed to ask what they think rather than tell them.

A child asks why we sleep, or whether a robot can think, and the Primer takes the question seriously: a checked explanation first, then the bigger question underneath, explored the way a good tutor would, by asking rather than telling, until the child can say what they think and why. It draws on thinkers from many traditions and does not give its own view where people disagree. Parents choose which topics are switched on. Who pays is the open question: parents, with no exam to prepare for.

Designed, not built. Nothing here has been tried with a child yet.

Foundations Ages 4 to 7

A coach for the adult teaching a young child to read, write and count.

A screen cannot guide a five-year-old's finger along a line of print, form a letter with their hand, or tell when they have had enough. A parent or teaching assistant can. Foundations keeps that adult at the centre of every session and gives them a teacher's know-how: the next step in a planned sequence, practice words using only sounds the child knows, what to listen for, and when to stop. It never listens to the child, never watches them and never rewards them for staying on.

If anyone pays, it is parents or schools, less urgently than for exams: the owner's view, still untested.

Designed, not built. The least certain of the three ideas, and deliberately not the first.

Which goes first

Foundations does not go first. The owner's reasons: a child of 4 to 7 needs a person beside them to learn to read and write, schools already teach it for free, and of the three ideas it carries the highest risk of not working Assumed (OA-28, OA-29). Between the Tutor and the Primer the choice is open. It will be made on what teachers, professors and parents say in the field research, against one rule written down in advance, and recorded in the decision ledger with what it rules out.

DecisionD42, 22 October 2026

What the three share

All three rest on one rule carried over from the Tutor: nothing reaches a child that cannot be traced to a source someone has checked, and what cannot be traced is blocked, or answered with an honest "not yet". All three refuse engagement tricks, with no streaks, badges or reminders; none acts as a friend; an adult is always accountable; and any piece of content can be withdrawn in one step. They share a platform, not a customer: the ages, the buyers, the data and the law all differ.

DecisionD40, D41, D44, D25

What happens next

Before anything is built, the owner will put each idea to the people who would have to believe in it: teachers who have taught these ages, professors who study how children learn and think, and parents who would pay. The questions are written to find out what would make each idea wrong, not to collect encouragement, and every answer is recorded against the assumption it tests.
