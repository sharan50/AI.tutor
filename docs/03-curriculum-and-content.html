Curriculum and content

Nothing reaches a learner that cannot be traced to a validated item. This document sets the clean-room rule that decides what may enter the item bank, the examiner process that validates it, the accuracy floor as a number, and what the product does when it falls below that number.

Scope at launch

GCSE Mathematics, Higher tier, Algebra strand, aligned to Pearson Edexcel. Roughly 60 objectives and roughly 600 items, every one examiner-validated before a single learner sees it. That is narrower than is comfortable and narrower than the brief's ambition, and the narrowness is the point: the correctness promise is only worth making where it can be kept.

Why this subject and tier

  • Mathematics, because the floor is falsifiable there. A worked algebraic step is either correct or it is not, and a symbolic checker can adjudicate most of them without a human. Teaching something wrong before an exam is the second-ranked risk in 12, and mathematics is the subject where that risk can actually be measured rather than asserted.
  • Higher tier, because the demand is there. Verified Year 11 is the peak tutoring year at 25% of pupils (Sutton Trust, Private Tutoring 2026, February 2026), and tutoring demand concentrates where a grade boundary is in play.
  • Algebra, because it is where the style engine has something to do. The dimensions in 02, point of entry, lateness of formalism, one running example or many, are precisely the axes on which algebra teaching visibly differs. A strand chosen for measurability that had no pedagogical variation would test P1 to P3 and leave P4 untested.

The counter-argument, which is real: mathematics is also where the free tools are strongest and where UK competition is densest. Verified Third Space Learning's Skye is voice-based conversational maths tutoring and is already in market. Launching into the most contested subject is a deliberate trade of competitive comfort for measurability, and it is recorded as such in the decision ledger.

Why Pearson Edexcel, provisionally

Content is written to the DfE-published subject content, which is the statutory source; the board determines assessment shape, command words and answer conventions, so a board still has to be named.

Launch alignment is Pearson Edexcel, for one reason: Verified AQA's published policy states that AQA does not permit the use of any AQA material, in any manner or for any purpose, in connection with the training of AI-powered tools or technologies, and that it will not allow use of AQA material in or on any edition of a third-party work where any part of that work is generated or produced using AI. Pearson's published position concerns reproduction of past papers and mark schemes by approved centres where students are not charged, which is a narrower and better-understood constraint.

We use no material from either board, so neither policy is engaged by our content on its face. Aligning to AQA would nonetheless put the launch strand behind the board whose published AI stance is broadest, and OI-1 (does the AQA clause reach runtime retrieval or only training) is unanswered.

Trigger to revisit: Unknown relative board share of GCSE Mathematics entries. It is not in the verified findings and has not been checked. If AQA's share is materially larger, this decision is wrong on commercial grounds and should be reversed once counsel has answered OI-1. What it makes harder: if we later add AQA alignment, command-word and answer-convention work is repeated per board.

Decision D1914 September 2026, and it is provisional

How the item count is arrived at

Not plucked. Derived from how the product is used, and the derivation is shown so it can be disagreed with.

  • A session covers one objective (01).
  • A Year 11 student revising one strand over a spring term at roughly three sessions a week for ten weeks completes on the order of 30 sessions, with repeats.
  • 60 objectives therefore gives approximately two terms of non-repeating material for the launch strand, with headroom for the student who works faster.
  • Each objective needs, as a design rule: one canonical explanation node, three misconception nodes, three in-session practice items and three held-out check items. Ten items per objective.
  • 60 objectives times 10 items is approximately 600 items in the launch bank.

The true objective count for the Algebra strand at Higher tier. It cannot be stated until the DfE subject content has been decomposed into objectives, which is the first content task and is not a research finding. 60 is a planning figure derived from usage above, not a count of anything. The decomposition either confirms it or it does not, and the item budget moves with it.

The clean-room policy

Decision D12. The operative sentence is short and everything else is procedure to make it true.

No exam board material enters the system, in any form, at any stage, for any purpose. Items are original work written to the DfE-published subject content.

What may and may not enter the item bank. There is one boundary case, named below the table rather than left for a reader to find.
May enterMay not enter, under any circumstances
Original items written by CINE writers working from the DfE-published subject content.Exam board specification text, in whole, in part, or paraphrased.
Mathematical facts and standard results, which are not copyrightable expression.Past papers, question text, or any question reconstructed from one.
Worked solutions authored by CINE writers.Mark schemes, grade descriptors, and examiner reports.
Misconception descriptions authored by CINE writers or contributed by validators from their own teaching experience.Textbook or third-party revision material, including material that is freely available.
Command-word and answer-convention descriptions written in our own words from publicly observable paper structure.Anything a validator has access to by virtue of being an examiner. See below; this is the sharpest edge in the policy.

The boundary case, stated plainly because an earlier draft said there was none. Command words, mark tariffs, paper structure and time allowances are observed from board papers we may not reproduce, and they enter the system as the paper specification and as item metadata. No board text is copied, and facts about a paper's shape are not copyrightable expression, so the copyright position is unaffected.

But it is not accurate to say nothing derived from a board paper enters the system. What is true, and what the clean room actually delivers, is narrower: the clean room defends the copyright position and does nothing at all for the naming and misleading-description risk, which is handled separately and less comfortably in 04. Conflating the two would let a strong copyright story stand in for a weak marketing one.

Boundary caseobserved structure, not reproduced text

The rule that matters most

A validator is engaged on terms that expressly forbid them bringing any awarding-body material into the work, and require a positive declaration on every validation record that they have not done so and are not in breach of their own confidentiality obligations to any board.

This is the sharpest edge in the clean room, and it points the opposite way to intuition. The people best qualified to validate our items are current examiners. A current examiner holds exactly the material the clean room exists to exclude, and holds it under confidentiality obligations of their own. An engagement that is casual about this does not merely risk our position; it puts the validator personally in breach. The declaration protects them as much as it protects us, and it is drafted into the engagement letter rather than mentioned in a briefing.

Decision D2014 September 2026

What follows in practice: validators work from our item and the DfE content reference. They are not asked "is this like a real exam question", because that question invites exactly the recall we have excluded. They are asked whether the item is mathematically correct, correctly placed in the specification, appropriate to the tier, and conventional in its notation and answer format.

The last of those five checks sits uncomfortably next to D20 and the discomfort should be admitted. Asking a current examiner whether notation and answer conventions conform to UK GCSE norms is asking them to draw on professional knowledge that they acquired, in part, from material they may not bring into this work. There is no bright line between "knows the conventions of the subject" and "remembers the papers".

What separates them in practice is the direction of the question. A validator may say this notation is wrong for GCSE. A validator may not say this resembles a question I have marked, and is instructed not to volunteer it. That is a thinner protection than the rest of the clean room and it depends on the validator's professionalism rather than on a control, which is why it is written here rather than assumed.

Thin pointprofessional knowledge against recall, D20

Screening

Attestation alone is weak, because an honest writer can reproduce something they read years ago. Two additional checks:

  1. Independent similarity screening through a third-party service that indexes publicly available material and returns a similarity score. We do not hold a corpus of board material to compare against, because holding it would itself breach the policy. Unknown Which service, at what price, with what coverage of UK exam material. Named as a procurement task at M2 in 11 Roadmap.
  2. Two-person rule. The writer and the validator are always different people, and neither can approve their own work into the live bank.

Provenance, recorded per item

Every item carries a provenance record that is written once and never edited. An item without a complete record cannot be live; that is enforced by T-VAL-01 rather than by policy.

Four fields are doing real work. derived_from must be either a DfE content reference or the literal string original; there is no free-text option, because a free-text option is where "adapted from" would eventually appear. structure_basis exists because derived_from could not express the boundary case above, which meant the provenance record, the centrepiece of the counter-notice pack, could not record where a mark tariff or a command word came from. A record that cannot describe its own hardest case is not a record. attestation is per item, not per writer, so it cannot be given once and forgotten. status is the only gate: nothing serves from the bank unless it reads live.

Examiner validation, and who performs it

Who. A current or recent examiner in GCSE Mathematics, or a teacher with at least five years of Higher tier teaching and documented examining experience. Engaged as a contractor under the terms in D20 above. Identity, qualification and awarding-body experience recorded against their validator ID, because a validation record that does not say who validated it is not a validation record.

What they check. Five things, in this order, and a failure on any one returns the item:

  1. Mathematical correctness of the item and of every step of its worked solution.
  2. Specification alignment: the item tests the DfE content point it claims to test, and nothing outside it.
  3. Tier appropriateness for Higher.
  4. Notation and answer conventions conforming to UK GCSE norms.
  5. For misconception nodes: that the misconception described is one that students actually hold, in the validator's own teaching experience.

Independence and sampling. Writer and validator are always different people. A 10% sample receives a second independent validation, and disagreement between first and second validator on any item triggers full re-validation of that writer's recent batch rather than adjudication of the single item.

Re-validation. On any change to the DfE subject content, on any change to board assessment conventions, and otherwise annually. revalidate_after is enforced: an item past its date drops out of live automatically. Content rot is a silent failure mode and a date field is cheaper than remembering.

Why the floors are not 100%

ACC-1 is 100% because it is a census of a process and a missing record is a fact, not an estimate. ACC-2 to ACC-4 are measured by sampling with human adjudication, and a 100% claim over a sample is not a claim anyone can test. A floor that cannot be falsified is worse than a lower floor that can.

The runtime gate is a separate mechanism aiming at zero, described in 06 and shown to the learner in 01. The floors describe residual risk after the gate, not the gate's tolerance. Conflating the two would be the easiest way to mislead a reader of this document, including a future version of ourselves.

The uncomfortable arithmetic of that floor

A 99.5% per-turn floor sounds strong. Per session it is not, and the honest number is the one a parent experiences.

Per-session risk is 1 - (1 - p)^n, where p is the per-turn floor and n is the number of tutor turns in a session. Unknown n is not known; it is measured at M2 and is marked unknown in 06 and 10 as well. So the honest presentation is the relationship, not a single number.

Chance that a session contains at least one unsupported assertion. Arithmetic, not measurement: every cell is 1 - (1 - p)^n. The column to read is whichever turns out to be the real session length.

Derived from ACC-2and it is not comfortable

Two things follow, and both are commitments rather than observations.

The per-session figure is the reported figure. Internally and externally, groundedness is reported as "proportion of sessions containing at least one unsupported assertion", not as a per-turn percentage. Per-turn numbers flatter the product by a factor of roughly twenty and are the number a competitor would quote.

No marketing claim in the form "it does not make things up" is made until ACC-2 is measured at or above 99.9% per turn on two consecutive weekly runs. Until then the product claim is the narrower and true one: every substantive statement cites a validated item, and the tutor refuses rather than guesses when it cannot. That claim is supportable at the launch floor; the stronger one is not.

Decision D2114 September 2026

This is the single clearest case in the repository of a number that would have been easier to leave at the per-turn figure. It is stated this way so that someone reading in six months cannot accidentally market a 99.5% floor as though it meant what it sounds like it means.

What it makes harder: we will run behind the current model, and we carry the cost of qualifying every upgrade.

Behaviour below floor

Two mechanisms at two timescales. Both are specified; neither has been built.

Runtime gate, per turn

A turn whose assertions are not supported by the cited node is never shown. The learner sees the refusal in 01, the tutor offers the examiner-written item verbatim, and the event is logged for adjudication. This is a hard block, not a confidence threshold, and it is the reason ACC-2 describes residual risk rather than tolerated risk.

Fleet gate, per week

When a weekly measurement of ACC-2, ACC-3 or ACC-4 falls below floor:

  1. The affected objectives are identified from the adjudication records.
  2. Those objectives are withdrawn from the live catalogue within 24 hours of the measurement completing.
  3. Learners with those objectives scheduled are moved to validated-item-only mode: the tutor presents the examiner-written explanation and worked solution verbatim and does not generate explanation for that objective. Teaching quality degrades; correctness does not.
  4. The objectives return to the live catalogue only after two consecutive weekly runs at or above floor.
  5. If more than 20% of live objectives are withdrawn at once, the whole strand goes to validated-item-only mode and the incident is treated as a product outage rather than a content defect.

Validated-item-only mode is the reason the item bank is authored as standalone readable material rather than as fragments for a model to assemble. The fallback has to be a usable product on its own, or it is not a fallback.

Practice papers, and the word "mark scheme"

A practice paper is original items assembled to the shape of the board's paper. Assembly is deterministic, it draws only from the validated bank, and it introduces no new content. That is what makes a paper checkable rather than merely plausible.

What "board-aligned" means here, precisely. The paper matches the structure a candidate will meet: question count, mark distribution across the paper, the mix of command words, topic coverage against the specification, and the time allowance. It does not reproduce, adapt or paraphrase any board question, and the structural description is written in our own words from publicly observable paper shape. The clean-room rule is unchanged, and D12 is not weakened by this feature; it constrains it.

Every paper ships with a mark scheme, and it is ours, for our items, validated by our examiners. Nothing in it comes from a board.

"Mark scheme" is a term of art that sounds like it belongs to an awarding body, and a parent skim-reading a marketing page would reasonably assume it did. So the product says "CINE mark scheme" in every learner-facing and parent-facing surface. This is a naming rule, not a preference, and it belongs with the board-naming convention in 04.

Decision D3614 September 2026

What this costs. Very little in content, because a paper is an assembly of items that already exist and already carry validation. It costs in metadata: every item now needs a mark tariff, a command word and a difficulty label, and those are set at authoring and checked at validation. It also costs a structural specification per board and per paper, which is the piece of work that has to be repeated if a second board is added, and is the real reason D19 matters.

validation_minutes_per_item and examiner_hourly_rate are both unknown. Neither is in the verified findings and neither is guessable to within a factor that would make the output meaningful; at 5 minutes an item against 25 minutes an item the launch cost differs fivefold, and the strategy differs with it.

How to get them: a timed pilot in which two examiners validate the same 20 items, measuring minutes per item by item kind, and a rate check against the contract market. Both are scheduled at M2 in 11 Roadmap and both are cheap. Why it matters beyond the launch bill: minutes per item is the constant that determines whether the content moat scales to a second strand, a second subject and a second board, and therefore whether the defensibility argument in 00 survives contact with a spreadsheet.

Unknownrequired input, and this is the one that decides whether the moat is affordable

What can be said without those numbers: the cost is linear in item count, item count is the only lever, and the design rule of ten items per objective is therefore the most expensive single decision in this document. It is set as a rule so that it can be argued with explicitly rather than drifting upward one item at a time.

Under what licence the DfE-published GCSE and A-level subject content documents may be used as the source for commercial item authoring, and whether that use requires attribution or carries conditions. The clean room depends on there being a lawful source to write from, and the entire content strategy rests on that source being the DfE's rather than a board's. Unblocks: the whole item bank. Cost of getting it wrong: the clean-room policy has no permitted source, and every item in the bank inherits the defect.
