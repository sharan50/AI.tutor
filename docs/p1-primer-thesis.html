Primer: thesis

The Primer is a tutor for a child's why-questions, for ages 8 to 13, on a tablet of its own. Its case begins with time, in the owner's view: every child asks why, and most evenings nobody has the time to answer properly Assumed. A child asks aloud or by typing; the Primer speaks a short answer at once while explainer videos are generated for the question, gives every fact with a source the child can see, and asks until the child can say where they stand, and why. On the owner's reading it is the idea an AI can do best, because conversation is what current models do well. The AI now also writes the answers and has their videos made, live: nothing reaches a child before it passes automatic checks, but most of it is never seen by a person first. It is also the idea whose buyer, reputation and proof are least established, and this document says so before anything else.

P2 Primer: product

The Primer Ages 8 to 13

A tutor for a child's why-questions, designed to ask what they think rather than tell them.

"Every child asks why. Most evenings, nobody has the time to answer properly." That is the owner's view, untested Assumed, and the Primer's case begins there. It is a tablet of its own, one per child, with nothing else on it. A child asks aloud or by typing; the Primer answers aloud at once while explainer videos, generated live for the question, play on the screen: facts with their sources, each checked by machine, then three positions from named traditions at their strongest, then questions until the child can say where they stand, and why. It gives no view of its own where people disagree, and on a choice in a child's own future it neither encourages nor discourages, setting out only the reality and what the schools of thought say. Parents choose which topics are switched on.

Who pays is the open question: parents, with no exam to prepare for, buying a tablet as well as the service.

Designed, not built. Nothing here has been tried with a child yet.

The Primer in four sentences

The Primer is a tutor for a child's why-questions, and it is its own tablet: one per child, with no browser, no other apps and no notifications. A child asks aloud or by typing, and the Primer speaks a short answer at once, at a high level, checked automatically before it is spoken; while it speaks, it writes and checks a script for each explainer, each on one specific concept, and has a video model generate the video from it, which plays once its checks pass. Every answer has one shape: facts, each cited to a source the child can see, retrieved for the turn from a reviewed allow-list, or to a reviewed entry, used first wherever one exists, with any sentence no source supports cut; then "the harder bit", the question underneath, set out as three positions from named thinkers or traditions, each at its strongest; then questions and evidence until the child can say where they stand, and why, which the Primer reads back. It never gives its own view where people disagree, never tells a child what to do, even about a choice in their own future, and never says "I", uses the child's name or praises; and an exchange closes with "Leave it there, or keep going?", so that the child decides when to stop.

DecisionD101, the owner's, 31 October 2026

Each part is a decision: the tablet D105 and its screen D109; what is spoken and what is shown D124; speaking D102, agreed at setup D129; the answer at once and the live explainer videos D122; each fact's source D125; the shape D110; the manner D108; the close D103, which runs within the session cap and never follows a safety reply, a stop or a decline (D115); and the limits on a child's own future D106. Speech becomes text at once and the audio is thrown away (D102). Counsel has cleared a child speaking to the Primer, and the recording of children's speech with consent for testing, in the UK and the US, as the owner reported on 4 November 2026 (D118). Typing is always there, and the owner expects about three in four questions to be spoken Assumed (OA-61, D123).

The Primer's case begins with time: "Every child asks why. Most evenings, nobody has the time to answer properly." It stands beside the case for asking rather than telling, as the owner's view.

DecisionD114, from the films, confirmed by the owner, 31 October 2026

The decision says where the case begins, not that it is true; it is how the owner's explainer film opens. That parents of 8 to 13 year olds find, most evenings, that they lack the time to answer their children's why-questions properly, and would pay for something that does, is the owner's view Assumed (OA-53), which PM1 asks parents. If it holds, it names what the Primer replaces: not a tutor's hour, as in the Tutor's case, but the answer at home that nobody had the time to give.

It replaces an answer, not a parent. The time argument can be read as a machine standing in for the adult who was too busy, and the design is built against that reading. The parent chooses the topics and sees the questions and the child's views (D46, D48); the Primer never says "I", never praises and forms no relationship with the child (D108, D104); and nothing in it asks a child to come back (D25). Whether parents hear the argument as help or as reproach is part of what asking them about OA-53 will show.

The Primer is narrower than "an AI that answers children's questions", deliberately. A general assistant already answers a child's question. As far as this vault knows, none is built to state no fact that a source on a reviewed list does not support, with the source on the screen, to withhold its own conclusion as a rule, to give a parent control of the subjects, to keep no relationship with the child and to run on a tablet with nothing else on it, all at once Assumed; the category has not been surveyed (below). Those refusals are the product.

The films are the design, and they are illustrative. The owner's films were made with AI and nothing in them has been built; the film on the front page is a rough cut, still in progress, of a further version, the owner's "Version B with the Primer"; where this vault says "the football film", it means Version C. Where a decision departs from a film, the page that applies it says so and why (D101). Four departures belong here. The films show an explainer building on the screen in time with the voice; in the design it is a video generated live for the question, with its own voice-over from its checked script, labelled on screen as made by AI and showing no real person and no child, which plays once its checks pass while the conversation goes on (D122, D126, P4). The films show the Primer's text arriving whole on a card; in the design a selection model chooses, for each checked sentence, whether it is spoken, shown or both, and the whole text is a tap away (D124, P2). The rough cut shows a child typing; the owner expects about three in four questions to be spoken Assumed (OA-61, D123). And the football film, Version C, ends on a rule for the choice, "bet hard on what you believe in", and spread your bets, as fund managers do. The design sets that rule out as one position among others, attributed, and asks where the child stands, because on a child's own future the Primer neither encourages nor discourages (D106, D110, P2). The film's figures, players and sources are its content, not facts in this vault (P3). The demonstration for adult testers (D70 to D79) predates the films and does not change; P2 lists where it differs.

Why an AI can do this now, and why at eight to thirteen

The Primer is built around what current AI does well; the Tutor is built around what it does badly. The Tutor's case rests on general models being unreliable about correctness, so the item bank and the verifier are the product (06). The Primer's rests on the opposite: holding a patient conversation, following a child's reasoning and asking the next good question is what language models do best. The owner's view is that this makes the Primer the lower-risk of the two new ideas on whether it can work at all. Since 4 November 2026 it also trusts the model further than the Tutor does: for any question outside named living people and the sensitive modules (D127), it writes the answer live from sources it retrieves and has its explainer videos generated live, with automatic checks only (D122, D125). So a catalogue written in advance no longer sets how much it can answer. What limits it now is what the reviewed sources can support (OA-32), and whether it is right turns on what the automatic checks catch (OA-62, RP10).

That a child of 8 to 13 can hold a typed or spoken conversation on a screen long enough for an inquiry to work, and that this is something current AI does well. The youngest band is the least certain: a nine-year-old types slowly, is misheard more often and tires quickly. The two halves are tested at different times: whether a child can sustain the conversation is observed in a human-run pilot at PM3, before the Primer is built; whether an AI can lead it well is tested only when a built Primer runs at PM4.

AssumedOA-23, the owner's view

Speaking removes slow typing, which is why children may speak from launch (D102), and why the owner expects about three in four questions to be spoken Assumed (OA-61, D123). Counsel has cleared it in the UK and the US, as the owner reported on 4 November 2026 (D118). It adds two things typing did not need: recognising a young child's speech, which is least accurate for the youngest Assumed (CV-11), and taking turns aloud in real time. That children speak readily, and are recognised well enough that the conversation does not stall, is Assumed (OA-55), tested at PM3 and PM4. The films set a brisk pace: one question takes three to six minutes, a design target (D112). The session cap stays a design choice set from PM3's pilot, and how soon the spoken answer must begin is a threshold to be written down before PM4 (T-PRM-14), Unknown today. How long a child then waits for the first video is observed at PM4, and is Unknown too (OA-62).

The Primer still needs correctness. A child's why-question usually has a factual part before a philosophical one, and a confident wrong explanation to a nine-year-old is worse than none. So facts keep the purpose of the Tutor's rule (D44) in a new form: every factual sentence cites a source the Primer retrieved for the turn from a reviewed allow-list of reference sources, or a reviewed entry where one exists, and is entailed by it; a sentence no source supports is cut, and a turn left with nothing to say gets "not yet" (D125, T-PRM-01 in P3). A reviewed entry is used first wherever it exists. A figure whose source is itself an estimate is said with "roughly" and keeps its source; a figure no source supports is never said (D113, P3), so "roughly" is never a way round the rule. Facts and stories about named living people, and every sensitive module, come only from reviewed entries and packs, never from live generation (D127). The explainers are held to the same rule. Each is a video generated live from a script the Primer writes and checks, so its voice-over and on-screen words come from checked content; its frames pass an automatic image check before it plays; it is labelled on screen as made by AI, and it shows no real person and no child (D122, D126, P4). That supersedes D111, and D73's rule that a video is never generated live for a child. Conversation is where the model is trusted; facts, figures and videos are where it is checked, by machine before a child sees them and by people afterwards, in a sample (D128).

Eight is where the band starts, not where the ambition stops. Below about eight, the owner's view is that a screen alone cannot hold a child's attention or teach the physical side of reading and writing Assumed (OA-28), which is why Foundation puts no screen in front of the child at all (D93). From about eight, in the owner's judgement, a child can hold a conversation, and that is what the band rests on (D41); now that a child may speak, reading and typing no longer mark the floor. The band is a decision, not a finding: Decision D41 sets Foundation at 4 to 7, the Primer at 8 to 13 and the Tutor at 14 to 18.

The tutors of history, and what the Primer takes from them

The ambition is an old one: a tutor for every child, of the kind that, in the two stories below, was kept for future rulers. One pair is Greek and one Indian; the Primer takes the form from them and nothing else.

Aristotle taught Alexander, beginning when Alexander was about thirteen. Chanakya, traditionally identified with Kautilya and credited with the Arthashastra, a treatise on government, became counsellor and adviser to Chandragupta, who founded the Mauryan empire; legend makes him a teacher at Taxila. Not every historian accepts the identification of Chanakya with Kautilya, and the dating of the text is disputed.

AssumedCV-02 and CV-03, awaiting the owner's check

Two things follow, both constraints rather than decoration.

  • The form, not the relationship or the views: one teacher, one learner, and a conversation that follows the learner's questions, which the Primer tries to make ordinary. The historical tutors followed a learner over years and knew their life; the Primer remembers a child's past questions and views as summaries, and nothing of their life (D104). Aristotle and Chanakya appear in it, if at all, as named thinkers whose positions are attributed to them, beside others who disagreed with them, and across each module the positions come from more than one culture, though not inside every question (D110).
  • The ambition, never a real person's voice. Putting words in a real person's mouth misattributes views, and a simulated historical figure talking to a child is the companion pattern 05 forbids (05). It would make the most memorable demonstration, and it is ruled out; for the same reason, no generated video shows a real person (D126).

The Primer names methods, never people: it may run a Socratic dialogue, a thought experiment or a story, and it never speaks as a real person.

DecisionD47, 22 October 2026

D47 is about whom the Primer speaks as, not whom it names: read with D44, it names a thinker whenever it attributes a position, and never speaks as one. The two positions a child did not choose ask back as positions, in the Primer's words and attributed, "A Stoic would ask...", never in a thinker's first person, and each ask-back's attribution is checked against a source retrieved for the turn, or a reviewed pack (D110, T-PRM-02). Real people may also appear as evidence: the bank may hold the documented stories of named people, living ones included, as the football film tells footballers' stories, using only public, documented facts from reviewed sources, never a private life, never quoted beyond the source and never voiced (D113), and such stories come only from reviewed entries, never from live generation (D127, P4). What duties that brings, in the UK and the US, is Open OI-30.

The age in the first pair is worth noticing. If the account is right, Aristotle began with a thirteen-year-old Assumed (CV-02): the top of the Primer's band, not the bottom of Foundation's. The novel that gives the Primer its name makes a related point.

In The Diamond Age, Nell's Primer is voiced live by a human actor, Miranda, who becomes a mother figure to her; the novel leaves open how much of Nell's development comes from the machine and how much from Miranda.

AssumedCV-04, awaiting the owner's check

The design takes that as a warning rather than a plan, and the films sharpen it, because this Primer speaks. Its voice is synthetic, no real person's, and carries no persona (D47). It never says "I", never uses the child's name and never praises (D108, tested by T-PRM-09), so that nothing in its manner stands in for a person. It forms no relationship with the child (D104), and it keeps the child's adults in the picture: they choose the subjects (D46) and see the questions and the child's views, as summaries (D48, D104).

What Y Combinator asked for, and where the Primer parts from it

Y Combinator's Fall 2026 requests for startups include one called "The Primer". It opens by observing that the best education has always been one-to-one tutoring, that Aristotle taught Alexander, and that the privilege was reserved for the few. It names the Primer of The Diamond Age as the long-term goal, and asks for adaptive teaching of reading, writing and arithmetic to young children, close to the quality of a private tutor, at consumer scale, supplementing teachers rather than replacing them, and starting as something parents buy for basic skills. Secondary write-ups give the ages as 4 to 10. The page itself could not be opened from the build environment; this is a paraphrase from search summaries of it.

AssumedCV-01, awaiting the owner's check

Where the Primer agrees, taking the request as CV-01 records it Assumed. One-to-one tutoring is the standard to aim at. The tutor supplements teachers rather than replacing them. Parents are the first buyers. And the long-term goal the request names, a tutor that teaches a child not only to read but to think and reason, is the Primer's goal exactly.

Where it parts, and why. As CV-01 records it, the request starts with reading, writing and arithmetic for young children, bought by parents for basic skills, and names the thinking Primer as the long-term goal Assumed (CV-01). The owner's order differs (D94, which keeps D42's). Separately from the order, in the owner's view a five-year-old does not sit still to be taught by a screen, reading and writing are taught physically by a person guiding a finger along print and forming letters with the child, and at that age managing the child is half of teaching them Assumed (OA-28). The thinking tutor for older children is, on that view, the part an AI can deliver now, though the tablet it would run on still has to be designed and made (D105); so the Primer starts with what the request names as its long-term goal.

Foundation does not go first. Rejected: the order of YC's request as CV-01 records it Assumed, which starts with basic skills bought by parents and names the thinking Primer as the long-term goal.

DecisionD42, 22 October 2026

This is a real risk, not a debating point: an application that argues with the request's sequence must do so openly, or it reads as not having read the request. Since 29 October 2026 the order rests on D94, the owner's order given without reasons, and D42's reasons are withdrawn with Foundation's design for a parent at home; which idea goes first says so.

What makes it different from free AI study tools

The free study modes already answer children's questions patiently and Socratically; the Tutor's thesis concedes that this behaviour is now a free toggle (00). The Primer does not compete on being a better conversationalist. It competes on eight things a general assistant is not built to do, five of them refusals.

Where the case is weak

Fourteen places, in the order a sceptical investor would reach them.

  1. Nobody yet knows why a parent would pay. The Tutor's strongest commercial argument is that a household already paying a human tutor before an exam is choosing between that hour and the product (00). The Primer has no exam and no deadline. The owner's answer is time: it replaces the answer at home that nobody had the time to give (D114). That is a hypothesis, not a finding Assumed (OA-53), and whether parents will pay at all is still OA-22, the Primer's most load-bearing assumption. Unknown the price, and what it would be compared with.
  2. The tablet has to be made, and bought. A parent buys hardware as well as a service (D105), and the comparison becomes a children's tablet, or an app on a tablet the family already owns. Whether parents will buy a dedicated tablet for one child, at a price that covers it, is Assumed (OA-54); what it costs to make, ship, support, repair and certify is Unknown; what a connected tablet sold for children must meet before sale, in the UK and the US, is Open OI-31; and a tablet that costs more than families will pay is RP7. It is also slower than software: at PM4 the Primer would run on a stand-in tablet locked to it, and the dedicated tablet is made only after PM4's evidence (P6).
  3. Live video's cost and speed are unknown. Each answer's explainer videos are generated live by a video model, while the child waits (D122). What that costs per session, and how long a child waits for the first video, are Unknown, and the register holds no figure for either. Whether video can be made fast enough for a conversation in real time, at a cost families will pay for, is Assumed (OA-62), measured at PM4. The spoken answer comes first so that a child is not left waiting; but if video is too slow or too dear, the explainers the films show cannot be made this way (P6).
  4. The reputational risk is larger than the Tutor's. An AI that discusses death, belief or right and wrong with a ten-year-old is a headline waiting for a writer, whatever the design says. Sensitive modules start switched off, their content comes only from reviewed packs and never from live generation (D127), and the Primer never gives its own view, which reduces the risk without removing it (RP1, OA-26).
  5. Most of what a child sees is checked only by machine. Nothing reaches a child that has not passed the automatic checks, but most of it is never seen by a person first (D122). People review a sample of the generated scripts and videos afterwards, and every one a child or parent reports (D128), which finds an error after a child has seen it, not before. Whether a sentence is supported by a source retrieved at answer time is a classifier's judgement, a gate, not a guarantee (D125). A live answer that tells a child something false is RP10.
  6. A generated picture can be wrong or unsuitable. A video model can invent, and a child may take a generated picture for a real one (D126). Every video is labelled on screen as made by AI, shows no real person and no child, and plays only once its frames pass an automatic image check; a video that fails is not shown, and the spoken answer stands. But the image check is a classifier whose error rate is Unknown (T-PRM-15), and a video cached by concept and shown to many children repeats any error it carries (RP10, P4).
  7. Reasoning about a child's own future is on by default. The Primer may reason with a child about a choice in their own future, such as whether to stake everything on football, setting out the reality and what the schools of thought say, and neither encouraging nor discouraging (D106). It is on at every age from 8 to 13 unless a parent turns it off, the reverse of the sensitive modules, and an AI setting out to a ten-year-old the odds against becoming a footballer is a second headline, whatever the fence says (RP9). Whether parents accept it on by default is Assumed (OA-57); whether it engages duties beyond OI-16's is Open OI-32. Questions about family, the body, feelings or safety still stop, with a reply shown and never spoken (D115, P4).
  8. The bank may name living people. The football film tells the stories of named footballers, and the bank may hold such stories, within D113's limits (above); they come only from reviewed entries, never from live generation (D127). A wrong or unfair sentence about a real person, told to a child, is a different harm from a wrong sentence about sleep. What duties apply, from privacy and defamation law to any rights in a name or image and the Children's code, in both markets, is Open OI-30.
  9. Counsel's go-ahead is known only as the owner reports it. Counsel has cleared a child speaking to the Primer, and the recording of children's speech with consent for testing, in the UK and the US, as the owner reported on 4 November 2026 (D118). The vault holds no copy of the advice: the written advice is to be filed with the DPIAs before any child speaks to the Primer, and an answer for the UK and the US is no answer for any other market. Speech is now the main way in, and the owner expects about three in four questions to be spoken Assumed (OA-61), so a misheard child (RP8) bears on most questions, not a few; and the audio is thrown away at once, so a mishearing cannot be checked afterwards.
  10. There is no proof that it works. The nearest human method, Philosophy for Children, was evaluated in the EEF's two trials: a smaller one found small positive effects, and a larger one found no evidence of an effect on attainment Assumed (P3, CV-05). "Thinks more critically" may be hard to measure at all (OA-24), and neither result may transfer to an AI-led version (OA-25). Nothing in this vault shows that an AI-led philosophical dialogue improves how children reason.
  11. The youngest children may not manage it. If a nine-year-old cannot sustain a typed or spoken inquiry, or is misheard often enough that the conversation stalls, the band starts later than designed and the market shrinks with it (OA-23, OA-55).
  12. A summary may misstate a child. What is kept of a conversation is the read-back the child hears at the close, in plain words, which the child can correct before it is kept; nothing is kept word for word, and the scripts and videos the Primer makes hold none of the child's words (D104, D117, D126). A summary that puts a view in a child's mouth, shown to a parent, is worse than no record. Whether children accept the read-back and parents understand it is Assumed (OA-56), tested at PM4.
  13. "Not yet" may still be common where people must review. "Not yet" now comes only when no source supports an answer, or a check fails (D125). But a question about a named living person, or in a sensitive module, gets "not yet" until reviewed content exists (D127), and the people who review the sensitive modules, the allow-list and the sample may become the bottleneck (RP5). A Primer that declines too often stops being asked. Whether the allow-list and the reviewed content can support an answer to most questions, and three positions, each at its strongest, be generated and attributed correctly for most, is Assumed (OA-32, OA-58).
  14. The category has not been surveyed. The register holds no survey of the products that already talk with children, free or paid, of the family settings the general assistants may now have, or of the children's tablets and AI devices a dedicated tablet would compete with. Until someone has read what they do and claim, the Primer's difference from them is asserted, not shown. That is a reading task at PM1.

One more weakness belongs to the market. Almost every user is under 13, and in the US, which the owner wants to enter alongside the UK (D51), AI chatbots that talk with children are under active regulatory scrutiny Assumed (P4, CV-10). Whether a design that refuses companionship keeps the Primer outside the new rules is Open OI-15, and a tablet that answers aloud looks more like a companion than text on a screen does; counsel's go-ahead on hearing a child (D118, above) does not settle it.

How it relates to the Tutor and to page 13

The Primer for 8 to 13 is a tablet of its own, so it cannot be a mode of the Tutor's app: page 13's gated mode, if it ever exists, is the Tutor's. Page 13, the Primer gateway, describes a Primer that grows out of the Tutor as a gated mode for its older students, once the Tutor has earned trust. Since D105 that cannot describe the Primer for 8 to 13, and D40's "may share one platform" means the back end: the pipeline, the checks and the kill switch that P5 reuses from the Tutor. Whether the three ideas become one product, a family's account that grows with a child, or stay three, turns on whether a child would move from one to the next, and nothing in the vault can yet say; it is set out in which idea goes first.

Page 13's principles and release gates bind the Primer all the same: agency over persuasion, epistemic humility, adult accountability, a bounded relationship and developmental fit (13), and the six gates an independent review must pass before release (13). Those gates are the Primer's milestone PM4.

Page 13's argument for older users, and the answer to it

Page 13 argued that the first Primer users should be older adolescents, who can say when they disagree, read their own transcript and opt out. At 8 to 13 those safeguards are supplied by design and by adults instead. Sensitive modules stay off until a parent switches them on (D46). Triage runs on every turn and stops any question about the child's family, body, feelings or safety (T-PRM-06); a choice in the child's own future gets the reality and the schools of thought, never a direction (D106). The Primer states no conclusion on a contested question (T-PRM-03). It remembers ideas, not lives, as summaries, with nothing word for word and no recording (D104), and the scripts and videos it makes hold none of the child's words (D126). The child hears the summary that will be kept and can correct it, this band's version of reading one's own transcript (D117), and the parent sees the topics, the questions and those summaries and, once a trained reviewer has judged it safe to tell them, a notice that a question was flagged and is being handled (D48, superseded in part by D104, D107 and D121).

What remains weaker is real. A nine-year-old is less able than a sixteen-year-old to notice being led, so T-PRM-04 has to be stricter, and the supervised pilot at PM4 is a condition of release. A child reasoning about their own future can be steered by a single sentence, which is why every reply in such a conversation is checked for direction (P4). And most of what a child sees is checked only by machine before it reaches them (D122), so page 13's adult accountability rests on people reviewing a sample afterwards, and on reviewed content where a mistake would do most harm (D127, D128). The full treatment is in P4.

Nor does the Tutor's evidence transfer: the findings in 00 are about AI supporting the teaching of a subject, and the Primer has to earn its own at PM4.
