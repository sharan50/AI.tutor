Primer: MVP build guide

The first thing built for the Primer is a demonstration, not the Primer: a web app that adults use while playing a child of 8 to 13. It answers any question within seconds, explains the answer in three building blocks, each with narration and a short video made for the question, and then asks the child what they think. The owner builds it with Claude Code in four or five days, from a specification published beside this page. It supports conversations with teachers, parents and accelerators. It shows nothing about how children engage or learn, and everything it generates is labelled as not checked.

What you are building

A tester opens an unlisted address, enters a passcode, and plays a child. They speak or type a question. A short answer starts to appear within seconds, and can be read aloud. Three building blocks follow, the ideas a child needs first to understand the answer: each shows its text and offers its narration at once, and plays a video when the video has been made. Then the demo offers a harder question and asks what the child thinks, a few turns at a time, until they say the answer in their own words. A question about the child's own life, their safety or a big subject gets a careful reply written in advance, and no video.

The mock-up shows every screen and each moment of a question. The specification says how to build them, and is written to be saved as CLAUDE.md in the demo's own repository, where Claude Code reads it first.

The Primer is shown first by a demonstration, used only by adults who play a child. It supports the conversations of PM1 and decides nothing about which idea goes first; it is not the built Primer of PM4, and no child uses it.

DecisionD70, the owner's, 25 October 2026

The demo answers any question, with no list of topics: flexibility over accuracy. Every answer, block and video says on screen that it is not checked. The Primer itself still says only what a reviewed explanation supports (D44).

DecisionD71, the owner's, 25 October 2026

No block can be skipped with an "I already know this" button. Every block is offered in full, and the child moves on by scrolling.

DecisionD75, the owner's, 25 October 2026

The words come first and the videos last, so the child always has something to read, hear or think about. The safety check, the short answer and the plan for the blocks start together; nothing is shown, and no video starts, until the safety check has passed.

DecisionD74, the owner's, 25 October 2026

The videos

Video models cannot be trusted with words, so the pictures carry the idea and the narration carries the words. Children of this age read slowly, which is why the owner made video the key part of the demo; but a model that draws a label wrongly or mouths a sentence badly teaches the wrong thing.

  • Pictures only. No words, letters, numbers or people in the picture; animals are allowed, never with human features. The clip's own sound is muted and the narration plays over it.
  • One style. Every prompt ends with the same instructions: bright, simple, flat animation, one continuous scene, a calm camera. The clips then look like one product.
  • One clip per block, eight seconds at 720p in 16:9, looping under narration that runs a little longer.
  • Made once. Clips are cached under the question's concept, so a repeated question plays at once and costs nothing. Before a pitch, ask your favourite questions to fill the cache.
  • A daily limit of sixty new videos, enforced by the app, because a provider's own cap can be overshot, Assumed (CV-20).
  • A graceful failure. A refused or failed clip is retried once; then the card shows a still picture drawn by the app, says the video did not work, and still speaks.
  • One interface. The video provider sits behind one function, so a different model can replace it in a day.

These rules bind the demo's videos: pictures only, one style, one clip per block, a cache by concept, a daily limit, one retry and a still picture, and the provider behind one interface.

DecisionD76, taken while writing, 25 October 2026

The Primer itself is meant to explain with narrated video as well as words. There a video is part of a reviewed explanation, made in advance, and never generated live for a child (P3).

DecisionD78, taken while writing, 25 October 2026

Safety, privacy and the testers

Testers ask the hardest questions first, so the safety check runs before anything is shown. It sorts each message, and every later reply in the thinking turns, into one of six kinds. Five get a reply written in advance and nothing else, and none reaches the video model:

  • The child's own life, their family, friends or feelings: a pointer to a grown-up they trust.
  • A death in their family: the same, said gently.
  • Their safety: tell a trusted adult today, and the staffed services, Childline in the UK, which 05 names, and in the US the Childhelp hotline and the 988 lifeline, which P4 names, Assumed (CV-12).
  • A big subject, such as religion or sex: a suggestion to ask the grown-ups at home.
  • Anything unsuitable: a plain refusal and an invitation to ask about how the world works.

A refusal by the model itself is treated as the last kind. The owner reads and approves every reply before the first tester, and the specification carries them as drafts.

Adults only. The welcome page says so before the passcode, and says what happens to a tester's voice and words. If a child does use the demo, stop the session: the conditions for a child in any Primer session are PM3's (P6), and the demo meets none of them.

Who sees what. Anthropic sees every question and everything written. Google sees spoken questions, the text it reads aloud and the video prompts; on the paid tier it does not use them to improve its products, Assumed (CV-20), and how long it keeps the audio is OA-43.

Safety in the demo: a safety check before anything is shown; replies written in advance for the kinds above, with nothing sent to video; adults only; a passcode on the site and on every paid call; and spending capped at each provider and in the app.

DecisionD77, taken while writing, 25 October 2026

The demo keeps no record of who asked what: no accounts, no audio and no conversation logs. It keeps the generated material in its cache, by concept, and timings and counts without content.

DecisionD79, taken while writing, 25 October 2026

What it does not have is as important: no modules or parent's switches (D46), no activity record for a parent and no trained reviewer behind a disclosure (D48). Those are the Primer's, and the demo shows none of them.

Before day one

Accounts and settings, done before any code is written.

  1. GitHub: a new private repository for the demo, with the specification saved in it as CLAUDE.md.
  2. Anthropic: a Claude Console account, an API key, credits, and the spend limit above, which the console lets an organisation set, Assumed (CV-21).
  3. Google: an AI Studio project on the paid tier, which needs a linked billing account and a prepayment, Assumed (CV-20); an API key; the spend cap above; and the video model's daily limit for the project, read from its limits page (OA-42).
  4. Netlify: a new site from the repository. Check that the account is on a credit-based plan, because background functions are not available on the legacy Free or Starter plans, Assumed (CV-22). Add the two keys, the passcode, a long random signing secret and the limits as secret environment variables, under the names the specification gives.
  5. The testers: a passcode, three to six adults, and a time with each.

If you use Claude Code on the web, its environment needs network access to the package registry and GitHub. The AI providers are called from Netlify's functions, not from the session, so test on the deployed site rather than inside it.

Running the role-play sessions

The sessions test the demo, not the Primer. Three to six adults, each for about half an hour: a parent, a teacher, and at least one person who has not seen the design.

  • The brief: play a child of a stated age, and ask five questions such a child would ask: two about the world, one about the past, one about numbers, and one hard one.
  • What to watch: how long until the first words; whether they read, listened or watched; whether the videos arrived before they moved on; what the thinking turns drew out; anything wrong, odd or unsafe.
  • Afterwards, three questions: what would you show a child, what felt like any chatbot, and what did you not trust?

Write the answers up with the field research, as views of adults about a demonstration, never as evidence about children.

What the demo cannot show

  • Whether children engage, persist or learn. Adults are playing them. That is OA-23's question, for PM3 and PM4.
  • Whether the answers are right. Nothing in it is checked (D71), and the Primer's reviewed bank (D44) is not in it.
  • The Primer's safety in full. No modules, no parent's record, no reviewer behind a disclosure.
  • The Primer's cost. Its cost per session is P5's, Unknown until PM4.

Show it as a demonstration, read the labels out to whoever is watching, and never let a child use it.
