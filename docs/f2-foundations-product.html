Foundation: product

A class, a teacher and a camera over every desk. The children work as they always do; Foundation reads each page as it is written and keeps the teacher's screen quiet until a child needs help, then says who, what happened and what to try. This document shows one lesson end to end, so that what Foundation does, and what it leaves to the teacher, can be judged rather than asserted.

The teacher and the class

The teacher is the only user. The children are the reason for it, and never use it.

Foundation is a tool for the teacher, sold to schools, not to parents and not for home use. It runs in the background of a normal lesson, and the teacher taps nothing during it. It replaces the earlier design, an adult coached through a session with one child, entirely.

Decision D89the owner's, 29 October 2026; supersedes D43 and D55 for Foundation

The teacher is a class teacher in Reception, Year 1 or Year 2, teaching the whole class in an ordinary lesson. They are trained and know their children; what they lack is a view of every page at once. Foundation does not tell them how to teach. It tells them where to look.

The children are four to seven (D41). They have no account, no login and no screen, and Foundation never addresses them. What they meet is an arm over their desk, which their teacher has explained to them, and their teacher, who comes over sooner than before.

The parents meet Foundation through the teacher. At parents' evening the teacher opens their child's term summary on the classroom PC and talks it through (below). Parents do not log in to Foundation, and nothing is sent to them by it.

There is no promise that children will learn more. That is the claim a school most wants to hear and the one this vault has nothing to support. The promises say what Foundation will not do, that its marking is right where computation can decide it, and that its reading and its alerts meet thresholds set in advance. Whether a teacher who knows sooner helps a child more is the question in F3.

The desk unit

On every desk, a thin white flexible arm rises from a hole at the edge furthest from the chair, curves over, and ends in a small camera angled down at the desk surface.

The desk unit watches the page and the hand only, never the child's face. It has no screen, no sound and no light; nothing on the desk shows the child anything. The desk units and the teacher's PC are hardware in the room; Foundation itself is software on the school's servers or in the cloud.

Decision D90the owner's, 29 October 2026; supersedes D50's "no camera" for Foundation

Three consequences of where the arm sits. It rises from the far edge of the desk, so the camera looks back towards the child's hands and down at the page, away from the face. It is flexible so that it can be set to each desk at installation, and is then locked, so a child cannot turn it towards a classmate or their own face; the view is checked at installation (T-FND-01, D97). And it is the same on every desk, so no child is singled out by having one.

The unit has no microphone. Nothing in the design needs to hear a child, and a child's voice raises the questions D50 deferred for the Primer (OI-17). That is a choice taken while writing, recorded with what is kept and not kept as D97.

The shape of a lesson

Before the lesson the teacher says what the class is working on. During it, the teacher teaches and Foundation watches. After it, what was read becomes part of each child's term.

  • Before. The teacher tells Foundation what the class is doing: a task from Foundation's library, sums from the board, or free writing. That is the only thing the teacher does with it for the lesson (D100).
  • During. The children work on paper, with pencils, as they would without it. The class view on the teacher's PC stays quiet: one tile per desk, each saying the child is working. When a child needs help, that tile turns bold and amber, and a small notice saying only "Foundation" appears in the corner of the screen, with a ping.
  • When the teacher goes over. The tile has already said what happened and what to try first. The teacher decides whether it is right. The tile goes quiet again when the child's next answer is right, or at the end of the lesson; the teacher does not have to clear it.
  • After. What was read is added to each child's term summary. Nothing else happens: no report goes home, and no child is told anything.

A tile turns amber on rules written with teachers, not on a model's judgement: an answer that is wrong with no working shown; the same mistake twice; a mistake at the same step of the working; a long stretch with nothing written; or a grip that has gone back to a fist. How long a stretch counts as long, and how many alerts a teacher can use in a lesson, are Unknown until teachers set them in the pilot (Assumed OA-50).

One lesson, end to end

Miss Smith teaches Year 1. On the board she has written 2 × 2, 4 × 6, 5 × 3, 8 × 3 and 12 × 2. Molly, who is six, sits at desk 7. The transcript follows Foundation's view of Molly's desk and the teacher's screen through the lesson the film shows. Nothing in it was produced by a system; it was written to illustrate the specification.

Foundation · Year 1, Miss Smith · times tables from the board · desk 7, Molly
Before the lesson

Miss Smith sets the lesson as "sums from the board" and starts teaching. She does nothing else with Foundation until the lesson ends.

The teacher's only step. D100.
Desk 7, what Foundation reads

Analysing. Molly writes 4 × 6 = 16. Checking answer: 4 × 6 is 24. No working on the page.

Computed, not judged. The answer is checked by computation (T-FND-03). The camera saw the page and Molly's hand; no frame is kept (T-FND-02).
The teacher's screen

Molly's tile turns amber: Molly: 4 × 6 answered 16. No working shown. A small notice saying "Foundation" appears in the corner, with a ping. Under the line: 16 can come from adding, 4 + 6 + 6, or from counting in fours and stopping early. Try: ask her to show you four groups of six.

Plain and factual, then a first triage. The first line is a template filled from what was read; the second is a reviewed entry from the error catalogue, chosen by a rule (D96, F3).
In the classroom

Miss Smith finishes what she was saying and goes to Molly's desk. "Show me four groups of six." Molly draws four rings and starts counting dots.

The teacher acts. Foundation said what it saw; Miss Smith decided it was worth the visit and what to ask (D93).
Desk 7, what Foundation reads

Molly crosses out 16 and writes 24. Checking answer: right. The tile goes quiet.

Desk 7, later in the week

Checking writing. Molly writes i love my dog spike. Capitals missed on "I" and "Spike"; the comma before "Spike" and the full stop missed; spelling right. Hand tracking: tripod grip.

Noted, not alerted. A sentence with its marks missing goes to the term summary and shows on the tile in normal type; it does not call the teacher over (F3).
Desk 7, a column sum

Checking working. Molly adds 16 + 8 + 19 in a column. The units make 23; she writes the 3 but carries 1 instead of 2, and her tens make 3, so she writes 33. The tile turns amber: Molly: 16 + 8 + 19 answered 33. The units make 23; she carried 1, not 2.

Where the working went wrong. Foundation points at the step, not just the answer, so the teacher knows what to ask before reaching the desk.
Parents' evening

Molly's parents sit with Miss Smith at the classroom PC. She opens Molly's term summary: times tables mostly secure, 4 × 6 once answered 16, which adding can give; carrying secure when adding two numbers, but with three she has carried 1 where the units made more than 19; capitals and full stops often missed; tripod grip throughout.

The teacher's record, in plain words. It describes Molly's own work, never ranks her against her class, and names no condition (D98).

Three things in the transcript carry the design. First, Molly never saw, heard or touched Foundation; what changed for her is that her teacher came sooner and asked a better question. Second, everything Foundation wrote was either a template filled from what it read or a sentence a teacher had written and another had checked. Third, the decision to go over was always Miss Smith's.

The teacher's screen

Foundation runs as a desktop app on an ordinary Windows PC on the teacher's desk: a quiet class view with one tile per desk. When a child needs help, the tile turns bold and amber with the detail, and a small corner notice saying only "Foundation" appears, with a ping. The wording is plain and factual, and offers an initial triage: what the problem looks like and a suggested next step. Foundation keeps a term summary for each child, which the teacher refers to on that screen at parents' evening.

The term summary

The term summary is the whole of what Foundation keeps about a child: what it read, lesson by lesson, and the patterns in it, in words a parent understands.

  • For each domain, maths, writing and reasoning, motor skills: what the child did, what they now do reliably, and the mistakes that recur, with when they were last seen.
  • For grip: how the pencil has been held over the term.
  • Nothing else: no images, no recordings, no notes typed about the child, no scores against the class, and no inference of a difficulty or condition (D98).

It is the school's record, kept for the school year and deleted when the child leaves the class, a design choice pending counsel (F4). The teacher may print it or read it aloud at parents' evening; Foundation does not send it anywhere.

What Foundation does not do

  • It does not teach. It tells the teacher where to look; the teacher teaches.
  • It does not address the child. No screen, no sound, no light on the desk (D90, D93).
  • It does not read faces. A frame with a face in it is discarded by the face check before anything else reads it (D97).
  • It does not keep what it sees. No image, video or audio is stored (D97).
  • It does not reward. No stars, streaks, badges or confetti (D25, D93).
  • It does not diagnose, rank or predict. It never names a condition and never compares a child with their classmates (D98).
  • It does not go home. It is not sold to parents and has no home version (D89).
  • It does not promise faster learning. Nothing in this vault supports that promise, so it is not made.
