Primer: product

One child, a tablet of their own, and one question at a time. The child asks aloud, as most are expected to, or types; the Primer speaks a short checked answer at once while explainer videos are generated, sets out three positions at their strongest, follows the child's real question with evidence, reads back where the child has got to, and asks "Leave it there, or keep going?". This document shows one question explored end to end as the film on the front page shows it, then a question in a subject the parent has switched off and one about the child's own life: the moments where a product like this is judged.

The child and their tablet

A child of 8 to 13 with a why-question and a tablet of their own, and a parent who pays, holds the switches and, the Primer assumes, has no time most evenings to answer properly.

Two children stand for the band. A nine-year-old who asks about everything, types slowly, and has a few minutes of real attention after school. And a twelve-year-old who argues back, has started to notice that adults disagree, and wants to know whether it is fair for everyone to get the same, or whether to bet everything on football. Each has the Primer, a tablet of its own with nothing else on it, one per child (D105, below), and either can ask aloud or type (D102). Counsel has cleared a child speaking to the Primer, in the UK and the US, as the owner reported on 4 November 2026 (D118). The owner expects most questions to be spoken (D123, below).

Three things follow, and each shapes the design.

  • They arrive with a question, not a subject. The unit of demand is "why do I have to do homework?", not "philosophy". The Primer is organised around questions and the modules they fall into, never around a syllabus. Choices in a child's own future have a module of their own, "Choices ahead": on at every age from 8 to 13 unless a parent turns it off, and outside the rules for sensitive modules (D106; P3).
  • They did not buy it, and they did not choose its limits. The payer is a parent, who holds the account, switches the modules and sees the summaries; the child chooses the question. As with the Tutor (01) they want different things, and the tension is sharper because the subjects are closer to what a family believes.
  • They tire and stop. A conversation that cannot end gracefully is designed for an adult, not a nine-year-old. Every exchange ends with "Leave it there, or keep going?", and leaving is one tap (D103).

The parent's side is time. The Primer's case begins there (D114): "Every child asks why. Most evenings, nobody has the time to answer properly." Version A of the films opens on those words, and shows a child's question met by the parents' answer, "good school, good job, good life". The Primer fills the time nobody has. It does not stand in for the parent, who decides what is switched on and sees where their child got to (below).

That parents of 8 to 13 year olds find, most evenings, that they lack the time to answer their children's why-questions properly, and would pay for something that does. The owner's view behind D114.

AssumedOA-53, resolvable by asking parents

That both children can hold a spoken or typed conversation long enough for an inquiry to work; whether an AI can lead one waits for PM4. The nine-year-old is the doubtful case.

AssumedOA-23, tested at PM3

Whether children of this age speak to it readily, and are heard accurately enough that the conversation does not stall, is OA-55, tested at PM3 and PM4.

The shape of a question

A question runs in real time, briskly. The child asks aloud, the main way in, or types; the Primer speaks a short answer at once while explainer videos are made, then goes on through three positions and the child's real question to the child's own position, read back, and "Leave it there, or keep going?". A session is one question, and its cap still cannot be extended.

  1. The question. The child holds the talk key and says it, or types it at the wake screen's one input box (below). Speech is the main way in, and typing is always there (D123). Speech becomes text at once and the audio is thrown away (D102). There are no starter questions.
  2. Clarify. If the question could mean two things, the Primer asks which.
  3. An answer at once. The Primer speaks a short answer at once, at a high level, from sources it retrieves for the question, checked automatically before it is spoken, with its source line and what is not known (D122, D125, below).
  4. Explainer videos. While it speaks, the Primer writes the script for each explainer, each on one specific concept, checks the script, and has a video model generate the video from it. Each video plays when its checks pass; one that fails is not shown, and the spoken answer stands (D122, D126).
  5. The harder bit. The question broken into three simplest positions, each from a named thinker or tradition and each at its strongest; then "Which is closest to what you think?" (D110).
  6. The other two ask back. As positions, never as people: "A Stoic would ask..." (D47).
  7. The real question. The one the child finds hardest to answer, put in their own terms.
  8. Probing. Questions, and evidence from the sources retrieved for the turn or from reviewed entries: figures, odds and documented stories, a named living person's only from a reviewed entry (D113, D127). On a choice in the child's own future, the fence below governs this step.
  9. The read-back. "You started with... Where are you now?" The child gives a position, with a reason, and the Primer reads it back in plain words. The child can correct it by saying or typing a change, and the corrected read-back is what is kept (D117).
  10. The close. "Leave it there, or keep going?", with two buttons of equal weight, "Leave it there" first (D103).

Every answer takes the whole shape, and the conversation goes on through it to the child's own view, read back (D110, D122). A sentence no source supports is cut, and a turn left with nothing to say gets "not yet": that comes only when no source supports an answer or a check fails (D125). Facts and stories about named living people, and every sensitive module, come only from reviewed entries and packs, never from live generation; until a reviewed entry or pack exists, a question that needs one gets "not yet" (D127; P3).

A child who wants to stop at any step says so, and the Primer goes straight to the close, reading back whatever position the child has given. The shape departs from page 13's dialogue (13), which draws out the child's view before any alternative: the films put the three positions first, and D110 follows the films (D101).

Every exchange closes with "Leave it there, or keep going?", and "Keep going" continues within the time cap.

DecisionD103, the owner's, 31 October 2026

"Keep going" carries the same conversation on from where the read-back left it, to a question one of the other positions asked back, say, or the next piece of evidence, and the next exchange closes the same way. It is offered once per exchange, at the close, beside "Leave it there", and is not an engagement device (D25). It never follows a safety reply, a triage stop or a decline, which close with "Leave it there" alone (D115). After a stop or a safety reply, the child's own words leave the screen at once, and the reply stays on it until the child taps "Leave it there", turns the screen off, or a short idle period passes, whichever comes first; the screen does not sleep on its own before then. The idle period is a design choice, its length Unknown, set with the specialist who writes the replies and weighed against the child's need to read the services named. Then the conversation closes and the screen returns to "What's your question?" with nothing of the exchange on it, which is all that anyone who wakes the tablet sees (P5). Session length is capped and a child cannot extend it, as 05 requires of the Tutor (05); at the cap the read-back comes and only "Leave it there" is offered. Unknown the cap for each age band: it is set from what PM3's human-run pilot shows about attention, not chosen now.

The conversation runs in real time and the Primer speaks briskly. One question takes three to six minutes, a design target. The session cap stays a design choice set from PM3's pilot.

DecisionD123, the owner's, 4 November 2026

Speech in. The owner expects about three in four questions to be spoken, Assumed (OA-61), measured at PM3 and PM4; the rough cut on the front page shows a boy typing (below). The verified adult agrees to speech when they set the tablet up, and it is on from the start once the spoken safety test has passed and the first supervised pilot has run; the child may speak or type at every turn, and the adult can turn speech off (D129). Counsel has cleared a child speaking to the Primer, and the recording of children's speech with consent for testing, in the UK and the US, as the owner reported on 4 November 2026 (D118; P4). No child speaks to the Primer until T-PRM-06's spoken battery has passed on the test recordings, and speech is first switched on for children in a supervised pilot, PM4's or one held to its conditions. The test recordings are made, with consent, under their own research protocol and DPIA, before any child speaks to the built Primer, and so before launch; none is made for the demonstration, which adults use (D119; P6). The weakest point of speech is a child misheard (RP8, OA-55).

The Primer's voice. Its own voice is a stock synthetic voice cloned from no one (D9) and no character (D47). A selection model chooses, for each checked sentence, whether it is spoken, shown or both, and the whole text is a tap away (D124, below). A safety reply and the reply to a triage stop are shown, never spoken (D115). The Primer speaks at the same brisk rate at every age (D112); whether that suits a child of 8 or 9 is for PM3's pilot to show.

The method is the shape. A story or a thought experiment sits inside it, as evidence in the probing or as the way a position is put. They are methods, not characters (D47), and the child does not choose between them.

When no source supports an answer, or a check fails, the Primer says "not yet" and turns the gap into the lesson: how could anyone find out? The question joins the writing queue, with no link to the child's account, as the Primer's own rewrite of it in general terms, held to the read-back's checks: no run of the child's words longer than a short phrase, and no detail of their family, friends, body or feelings (P3). How often that happens is Unknown until PM3 (D125, OA-32).

What an answer looks like

Every answer has one shape. The Primer speaks a short answer at once while explainer videos are generated live, every fact cites a source it retrieved or a reviewed entry, and the sources show on a small grey line beneath.

Every answer has one shape: a checked fact with its source; "the harder bit"; the question broken into three simplest positions, each from a named thinker or tradition and each given its strongest case; "Which is closest to what you think?"; the other two ask back, as positions and never as people ("A Stoic would ask..."); the child's real question; probing with questions and evidence; the child's own position, with a reason, read back. Positions come from more than one culture across each module, not inside every question. D47 stands: the Primer never speaks as any real person.

DecisionD110, from the films, confirmed by the owner, 31 October 2026

Three positions, each at its strongest. Each names the thinker or tradition and gives the case as its own holders would make it. A reviewed inquiry pack is used first wherever one exists; otherwise the positions are generated from the sources the Primer retrieves for the turn. An attribution its source does not support is cut, and one found in a release's sample blocks the release (T-PRM-02, D125). In a sensitive module, positions come only from reviewed packs (D127). The two the child did not choose ask back in the third person, never in a thinker's own voice; where a film has a thinker ask in his own name, this page rewrites the turn (below). Range is a module's job, not a question's. Whether three such positions can be generated and attributed correctly for most questions, and written and reviewed for the sensitive modules, is OA-58 (P3).

An answer at once, and videos while it speaks. The Primer speaks a short answer at once, at a high level, from sources it retrieves for the question, checked automatically before it is spoken. While it speaks, it writes the script for each explainer, each on one specific concept, checks the script, and has a video model generate the video from it; each video plays when its checks pass (D122; P5). The conversation then goes on through the rest of the shape, to the child's own view, read back (D110). Version C shows its explainers building in time with the voice: dots thinning from the many to the few, three columns for three positions, a staircase, a two-by-two grid of footballers, a stack for a hedge. In the product each is a generated video, so that any question a child asks can have one, except in a sensitive module or about a named living person, where nothing is generated live (D127). Explainers made in advance would cover only what was written beforehand. A turn that D127 holds to reviewed content shows no generated video, cached or new, and a decline, a triage stop and a safety reply carry no video.

What a video may show. Every generated video is labelled on screen as made by AI and carries provenance metadata. It shows no real person and no child; its voice-over and on-screen words come from its checked script; its frames pass an automatic image check before it plays; and a video that fails is not shown, and the spoken answer stands (D126; P4). Scripts and videos hold none of the child's words. A video may be cached by concept and reused, and the kill switch reaches every cached copy.

Who checks. Nothing reaches a child that has not passed the automatic checks, but most of it is never seen by a person first (D122). People review a sample of generated scripts and videos after the fact, and every one a child or parent reports (D128; P3). This is the weak point, and it is plain: a live answer or video can tell or show a child something false or unsuitable, and no person sees it first (RP10). Whether videos can be generated fast enough for a conversation in real time, at a cost families will pay, with checks that catch what is wrong or unsuitable for children of 8 to 13, is OA-62, measured at PM4. The cost and speed of video generation are Unknown.

Grounded in sources. Every factual sentence cites a source the Primer retrieved for the turn from a reviewed allow-list of reference sources, or a reviewed entry where one exists, and is entailed by it; a reviewed entry is used first wherever it exists. A sentence no source supports is cut, and a turn left with nothing to say gets "not yet" (D125; P3). The bank holds the evidence a choice needs: careers data, odds and the documented stories of named people, living ones included. Facts and stories about named living people, and every sensitive module, come only from reviewed entries and packs, never from live generation (D127). A living person is described only by public, documented facts from reviewed sources, never their private life, never quoted beyond the source and never voiced. A figure whose source is itself an estimate is said with "roughly" and keeps its source. Evidence follows the child's market: UK figures and cases for a child in the UK, US ones in the US (D113; P3). What the law asks of telling children the stories of named living people is Open OI-30 (P4).

The source line. Under each answer's checked fact, a small grey line under a rule: "Based on: ...", built from the sources the Primer retrieved for the turn, or from the reviewed entry the fact comes from (D109, D125). It is always shown (D124). An entry's identifier is for reviewers, and the child never sees it.

Spoken, shown, or both. The Primer writes its content first, and the voice, the videos and the words on the screen all come from it, so no captions are needed. A selection model chooses which checked sentences are spoken and which are shown; figures and the source line are always shown, and the whole text is a tap away (D124, below).

The screen and the tablet

The Primer is a tablet of its own, one per child, with nothing else on it. Its screen holds one question at a time. The talk key comes first and the keyboard second, and the tablet hears the child only while the talk key is held. A selection model chooses what is spoken and what is shown, and every video says it was made by AI.

The Primer is its own tablet, as every film shows: one per child, with no browser, no other apps and no notifications.

It is to be designed in parallel with the service and made only after PM4's evidence; PM4 is to run the Primer on a stand-in tablet locked to it (P6). Its lock, updates and security are P5's. The talk key first, the keyboard second. Speech is the main way in, so the talk key comes first: the child holds it, says the question and lets go. The keyboard comes second and is always there, for a child who would rather type or has been misheard (D123). The rough cut on the front page shows a boy typing; the owner expects most questions to be spoken (above).

DecisionD106, the owner's, 31 October 2026

The rule. When a child asks about a choice in their own future, what to aim for, whether to stake everything on one thing, which path to take, the Primer does two things and no more. It sets out the reality, from reviewed evidence with its source: the odds, and the documented routes people took, said "roughly" where the source is an estimate (D113). And it sets out what the different schools of thought say about the choice, each at its strongest (D110). Then it asks where the child stands, and reads their position back. It never says what the child should do, never predicts what will happen to this child, never judges their chances from anything they say about themselves, and never praises, warns or reassures (D108). Where the evidence describes what happens to most people, the Primer says so: it is the reality, not a verdict on the child.

What still stops. A question about the child's family, their body (eating, weight, injury, health), their feelings (distress, "I'm no good at anything") or their safety starts no inquiry, whenever it arises, including part way through a conversation that began as a choice. A choice that comes wrapped in a family matter, such as a parent forbidding it or trouble at home, is a question about family. The Primer says plainly that this is something to talk through with a person, and points the child to a trusted adult and, in the UK and the US, to the services P4 names. The reply is shown and never spoken, and no "Keep going" follows it (D115), because the films are set in a kitchen and the parent can be the risk (D26). If the exchange is flagged, it goes to the safeguarding review (D107).

Triage on every turn. Triage sorts each turn, not only the first, because a conversation about a choice can turn (P5). Where it is unsure whether a turn is a choice or a question about family, the body, feelings or safety, it stops. A check on every reply in such a conversation blocks any sentence that tells the child what to do, predicts their own outcome, or encourages or discourages them. T-PRM-06 tests both halves of the fence, and P4 sets the same fence against the law.
