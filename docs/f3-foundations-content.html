Foundation: content and evidence

Foundation's content is not lessons. It is what Foundation needs to read a young child's work and say something useful about it: the tasks a teacher can set, the rules that decide when a child needs help, and a catalogue of the mistakes children of this age make, with what each suggests and what to try. All of it is written by teachers and checked by other teachers; where computation can check it, computation does. Whether any of it helps a child learn is a separate question, on which the evidence register is silent.

The domains it reads

Four domains, in the owner's words: maths, verbal and non-verbal reasoning, and motor skills (D89). Writing sits across two of them, as language and as a hand moving a pencil.

Foundation does not set the order in which a class learns anything. The school does. Before a lesson the teacher says what the class is working on (D100), and Foundation reads the work against that. This removes the clash the earlier design had to manage between a home product's order and the school's (D55, superseded for Foundation): there is no second order to clash with.

Maths

Number facts, times tables, adding and taking away, and column methods with carrying and exchanging, as far as Year 2 goes. Foundation reads what the child writes, digit by digit, and checks it by computation, never by a model: 4 × 6 is 24 whatever a model thinks, and a verdict that disagrees with the computation is never shown (T-FND-03, the Tutor's computer algebra check carried over, F5).

The working matters more than the answer. In a column sum Foundation checks each column as it is written, so it can say where a mistake happened: in 16 + 8 + 19 the units make 23, and a child who carries 1 instead of 2 gets 33. That sentence, not "wrong", is what lets a teacher go to the desk knowing what to ask. An answer with no working is itself information, and the rules treat it as such: a wrong answer with nothing written beside it is the clearest case for an alert.

Writing and motor skills

Writing is read twice: as language, and as a hand at work.

  • As language. Letters in order as they are written; capitals at the start of a sentence and for names and "I"; full stops and commas; spelling, against the target where the teacher set a dictated sentence, and against a reviewed word list in free writing. "i love my dog spike" is right in its spelling and missing four marks: two capitals, a comma and a full stop.
  • As a hand at work. Where each letter starts and which way the pencil travels, reversals such as b for d, size and spacing; and the grip: which fingers are on the pencil, tripod or fist, and pressure.

Grip and pressure are the least certain things Foundation claims to read. A grip is visible from above; pressure, at best, shows indirectly in the line and the posture of the fingers. The design treats both as provisional: if T-FND-04 shows pressure cannot be read reliably, Foundation stops reporting it rather than guessing (Assumed OA-49).

Most of what writing shows is noted, not alerted. A missed capital goes to the term summary and appears on the tile in normal type; it does not call the teacher across the room. A fist grip that returns after weeks of tripod is the kind of change the rules alert on, because it is cheap to correct early and costly later. Which changes earn an alert is set with teachers in the pilot.

Verbal and non-verbal reasoning

Reasoning is read through printed tasks from Foundation's library: for verbal reasoning, tasks with words, such as finding the odd one out or the word that completes a pair; for non-verbal reasoning, tasks with shapes, such as continuing a pattern or choosing the matching shape. The child circles, ticks or draws; Foundation reads which answer was marked and compares it with the task's key.

This is the least developed domain in the design. A mark is easier to read than handwriting, but a task where the answer is a choice says little about how the child got there, and the catalogue for it is thinner. It is described here because the owner includes it; its tasks, and whether teachers of this age set them at all, are questions for FM1 (F6).

The tasks, the rules and the error catalogue: who writes and who checks

Every word the teacher reads is either a template filled from what was read, or an entry from a catalogue written and checked by teachers. Nothing is generated (D96).

The error catalogue is the content that matters most. For each kind of task it lists the wrong answers children of this age commonly give, what each suggests, and a first thing to try. 4 × 6 = 16 suggests adding, 4 + 6 + 6, or counting in fours and stopping early; the suggestion is to ask the child to show four groups of six. A rule matches each entry to what was read; when nothing matches, the tile says what happened and offers no triage, rather than guessing.

The writer and the checker are never the same person, as in the Tutor's item bank, and every entry carries its provenance in the form 03 specifies. The uncomfortable column is the same as before: the triage a teacher reads is the part most likely to matter and the part with the weakest check. Its cost, like the Tutor's validation, is a rate-limited human process, Unknown until FM2 measures it.

What may not enter

The clean room on the pattern of D12, adapted to a product that reads children's work instead of teaching from items.

May enterMay not enter
Tasks, catalogue entries, rules and templates written by our writers.Any commercial maths, phonics, handwriting or reasoning scheme's materials: worksheets, characters, mnemonics or its sequence copied as a sequence.
Number facts, spelling and punctuation, as facts of the language and of arithmetic.Text from children's books in copyright.
Department for Education material only on the terms OI-9 establishes.Any frame from a classroom. Nothing seen is kept (D97), so no child's page or hand is ever used to train or tune a model.

The last row has a cost. The models that read handwriting and hands must learn from examples of children's writing and hands, and the classroom will never supply them. They come instead from work collected separately, with parents' consent and under the ethics protocol in F4, or from sources whose terms allow it. Which, and at what cost, is Unknown, and it is a line in F6.

When a teacher sets sums from the board or tasks from their own scheme, Foundation reads the children's answers to them; it holds nothing of the scheme. That mirrors 03's boundary case for observed paper structure, and is a thinner position than the rest of the clean room, written here rather than assumed.

Reading a child's answer to a task the school set is reading the child's work, not reproducing the school's material.

What the evidence says, and what it does not

The evidence register holds nothing on how children of four to seven best learn maths, writing or reasoning; nothing on whether a teacher who sees a mistake sooner helps a child more; and nothing on how well a camera can read a young child's page or hand. That is stated first so that it cannot be missed.

  • CV-11 says speech recognition is least accurate for the youngest children. It supports leaving the microphone out; it says nothing about reading handwriting from a camera. Assumed
  • CV-09 describes England's expectations for early reading. Foundation no longer teaches reading, so it bears only on what a Reception or Year 1 teacher is working towards. Assumed
  • The evidence for AI tutoring in 00 concerns much older learners and a different product, and does not transfer.

So the design rests on two assumptions, and should not be presented as resting on anything stronger: that teachers can act on what Foundation shows them during a lesson (OA-50), and that a mistake caught in the lesson is worth more than the same mistake caught when the books are marked. The first evidence task is reading, the cheapest task in the plan: the published research on assessment during lessons in early years, on handwriting and grip at this age, and on recognising young children's handwriting by machine. No figure from that literature appears here, because none has been read and registered, and the vault does not fill gaps from memory.

Does a child learn more? Foundation cannot answer this about itself and should not try. The term summary describes what children did; it is not evidence that they did better because of Foundation. Proof needs an independent measure chosen with evaluators at FM3, an assessor who is not the teacher, and a comparison with classes taught without it, under the ethics protocol in F4. Until that exists, Foundation can promise readings that pass their checks and alerts teachers judge useful, and nothing more, which is what F2 promises.
