What this is for

Before anything is built, the owner will put each idea to the people who would have to believe in it: teachers who have taught these ages, professors who study how children learn and think, and parents who would pay. The questions are written to find out what would make each idea wrong, not to collect encouragement, and every answer is recorded against the assumption it tests.

Two decisions wait on these conversations: which idea goes first between the Tutor and the Primer (the decision rule); and whether the three ideas belong together as one product (one product or three). Two questions are not decisions: whether the Primer as the owner's three films show it (D101) survives contact with the parents, teachers and professors of children of 8 to 13, and whether Foundation's classroom design survives contact with the teachers, heads and parents of a class that would have it. Foundation's place in the order is the owner's (D94) and is not tested here.

The conversations also settle some assumptions outright. Of the vault's assumptions, twenty-two are resolvable by reading or by asking rather than by building (14). The ones that need asking are here: OA-10, OA-17 and OA-40 for the Tutor, and OA-35 for its US launch, with an informed prior on OA-21; OA-22, OA-26, OA-53, OA-54, OA-57 and OA-60 for the Primer; OA-28, OA-51 and OA-52 for Foundation. Others, such as OA-23, OA-55 and OA-50, cannot be settled by asking but can be given an informed prior, which is worth having before a pilot is designed around them.

This is the first milestone of each idea: M1 for the Tutor, PM1 for the Primer (P6) and FM1 for Foundation (F6). None of it requires an engineer.

Who to talk to

Former teachers are the natural place to start, and the owner has them. They are also the people most likely to be kind. Ask each one for an introduction to someone who does not know the owner, and weight what strangers say at least as heavily.

How to ask

Eight rules. The first two matter most.

  1. Ask about what people have done, not what they think of the idea. "Tell me about the last time a child asked you a question you could not follow up" produces evidence. "Would you use an AI that answers children's questions?" produces politeness.
  2. Describe the idea last. Ask about the problem first, so the answers are not shaped by the pitch. When you do describe it, ask what would make them say no, and who they think would hate it.
  3. Say that nothing is built. It is true, and it changes how freely people criticise. A film is shown only after the questions about the problem, and only as what it is: illustrative, made with AI, a design and not a working product. The Primer's, on the front page, is a rough cut and is introduced as one.
  4. Count commitments, not compliments. An introduction, an offer to pilot, a deposit or a request to be told when it exists is evidence. "That sounds amazing" is not.
  5. Ask everyone the core questions in the same words, so that answers can be compared across people and across ideas.
  6. Talk to adults only. This guide collects no information about any child: no names, no ages beyond the year group, no stories that identify a child. Any later session with children, such as the human-run Primer pilot at PM3 or the Foundation pilot at FM3, needs its own written protocol, informed parental consent and safeguarding arrangements, and is out of scope here.
  7. Record against the ids. Every answer is noted with the date, the person's role, the assumption or question it bears on (OA-22, OA-51 and so on), what was said, and whether it supports the vault's position or cuts against it.
  8. Write down what surprised you on the same day. Surprises are the answers that most often change a plan, and the first to be forgotten.

The Primer

The Primer's questions ask whether the design the owner's three films show is wanted, accepted and paid for. Several are written to prove it wrong, and an answer that does belongs in P1. Describe it plainly before asking, and say that nothing is built: a tablet of its own for one child, with nothing else on it. The child asks a why-question aloud, holding a key down while they speak, or by typing. It answers aloud while a simple drawing builds on the screen: first a checked fact and where it comes from, then the harder question underneath, set out as three positions from different traditions, each put at its strongest, then questions until the child says where they stand, which it reads back to them. It can do the same with a choice in the child's own future, such as whether to stake everything on football: it sets out the odds and what different schools of thought say, and never tells the child what to do, for or against. A question about the child's family, body, feelings or safety starts no conversation: it points the child to a trusted adult. It never says "I", never uses the child's name and never praises. It keeps a short summary of what the child asked and concluded, never a recording, and a parent chooses which topics are switched on and sees the summaries.

For the interviewer, not the description: it follows D104 to D106, D108, D110, D111 and D117, and the spoken questions go ahead of counsel's answer on whether a child's voice is biometric data (OI-17) under D102, so say nothing that suggests that question is settled.

Foundation

Foundation's questions ask whether its classroom design is wanted, accepted and paid for. They do not test its place in the order, which is the owner's (D94). Several are written to prove the design wrong. If they do, the honest response is to say so in F1 and change the design, not to explain the answers away. Describe the desk unit plainly before asking: a small camera on an arm that sees the page and the pencil, never the face, keeps no pictures, and tells only the teacher.

What to do with the answers

  1. Keep one log. A row per answer: date, role, the id it bears on, what was said, and whether it supports or cuts against the vault's position. The log is the evidence; a summary written later from memory is not.
  2. Re-read the tests every few conversations. Hold the answers against which idea goes first as they arrive, rather than at the end, when the most recent conversation will weigh most.
  3. Write answers into the register. When an assumption is answered, record the answer and the date beside it in 14. The marker changes only when the owner changes it.
  4. Record the decision. When the field research decides which idea goes first, or changes the Primer's or Foundation's design, the decision goes into the ledger with what was rejected and what it makes harder, as every decision in this vault does.
  5. Say what went against you. An answer that cuts against the owner's view is the most valuable kind, and the one an investor will ask about. It belongs in the document it affects, in plain words.
