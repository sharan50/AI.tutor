Primer: safety, privacy and regulatory

The Primer's users are 8 to 13, its subject invites the questions children find hardest, it is a tablet that hears a child speak, and one launch market is the US, where AI chatbots that talk with children are under scrutiny. The design answers with adult control, a strict refusal to be a companion, speech turned into text at once and never kept, summaries rather than records, a fence around a child's own future, and triage that stops an inquiry the moment a question is about the child's family, body, feelings or safety. Its counsel questions, OI-13 to OI-18, OI-20 and OI-30 to OI-32, are open and none is answered here; the Primer goes ahead of one of them, OI-17 on a child's voice, by the owner's decision (D102).

The position in one paragraph

The Primer is a one-to-one tutor on its own tablet, one per child (D105): nothing one user says reaches another, the condition the Tutor's Online Safety Act position rests on (05, Open OI-4), and the parent request channel keeps it (Open OI-18). A verified adult holds the account, on the Tutor's age assurance (05, Open OI-5), and sets up the child's tablet. Almost every user is under 13, so the rules on a child's consent to online services likely bear on the design Assumed (CV-07), and the lawful basis is Open OI-13. The tablet has no camera and listens only while the talk key is held; a child's speech becomes text at once and no recording is kept (D102, D116), ahead of counsel's answer to Open OI-17. The Primer remembers ideas, not lives, as summaries (D104), has no engagement objective (D25), and is not a companion. It may reason with a child about a choice in their own future, and never tells them what to do (D106). In the US, the amended COPPA Rule and new state laws on companion chatbots may apply Assumed (CV-08, CV-10): Open OI-15. Three questions are new since the films: the stories of named living people, Open OI-30; what the tablet must meet before sale, Open OI-31; and reasoning about a child's own future, Open OI-32.

The films are the owner's, illustrative and made with AI. Where this page says what they show, it describes the design, not a working system.

The tablet, one per child

The Primer is its own tablet, one per child, with nothing else on it (D105), so much of its safety is built into the object. What a connected tablet sold to families as a child's learning device must meet before sale, in the UK and the US, is Open OI-31; the rules below are what counsel will be asked about, each a candidate awaiting the owner's check, and none answers OI-31.

The Primer is its own tablet, as every film shows: one per child, with no browser, no other apps and no notifications.

DecisionD105, the owner's, 31 October 2026
  1. A verified adult sets it up. A new or wiped tablet reaches the wake screen only after a verified adult has passed the Tutor's age assurance and created the child's profile on it, with the age that sets the band (D57). There is no path by which an unverified person sets one up. From the age check the company receives a result, never an image or a voice (05); the Primer's own handling of a child's voice is D102's, ahead of counsel's answer to OI-17 (below).
  2. It is one child's. The wake screen, "What's your question?", needs no choice of child, because the tablet serves one. It cannot tell who is holding it and does not try, since nothing is inferred from a voice: a sibling who picks it up meets that child's band and that child's remembered questions. That is a limit, stated, and one reason the wake screen shows nothing of past conversations.
  3. Nothing else is on it. No browser, no other apps and no notifications (D105). It never lights, sounds or shows a message to bring a child back (D25), and wakes only when the child wakes it.
  4. No camera, and a microphone live only while the talk key is held, with the screen showing it (D116). T-PRM-13 checks both, by inspecting every hardware revision and by a software check of every path that can open the microphone, run on each of PM4's stand-ins before any child uses it and on every software release, the stand-in's own system updates included. On PM4's stand-ins, the system's own updates are held by the device management until that check has passed on them (P5).
  5. The parent's screen is elsewhere. The switches and the record are reached through the verified adult's own sign-in, on their own phone or computer, never on the child's tablet, so the child cannot change a switch or read the record. A change reaches the tablet before the child's next turn (P5).
  6. It keeps no conversation. Once a conversation closes, nothing of it stays on the tablet (T-PRM-10). The tablet holds which profile it serves, its identifier, its software version and its error events. The adult can wipe a lost or sold tablet remotely, closing the account wipes its tablets, and a wiped tablet returns to setup.
  7. It is kept secure as if the UK's rules below apply: a password unique to the tablet or set by the adult, a point of contact for security reports, and a published minimum period of updates (P5).

The rules counsel will be asked about

The UK's security rules for connectable consumer products (SI 2023/1007, under the Product Security and Telecommunications Infrastructure Act 2022) took effect on 29 April 2024. They require passwords unique per product or set by the user, a point of contact for reporting security issues, and a published minimum period of security updates. GOV.UK's guidance, as recorded in January 2024, excepts tablets that cannot connect to mobile networks, unless they are designed exclusively for children under 14.

AssumedCV-36, read through search summaries, awaiting the owner's check

Whether that exception reaches the Primer's tablet, made for children of 8 to 13 and set up on it by an adult, is part of OI-31. The design assumes the rules apply.

Standard 14 of the ICO's Children's code, "Connected toys and devices", asks a provider of a connected toy or device to include effective tools to enable conformance to the code. The ICO's guidance recommends that no personal data be collected while a device is in listening mode, and that the device show clearly when listening mode is on.

AssumedCV-37, read through search summaries, awaiting the owner's check

GOV.UK's statutory guidance on the Radio Equipment Regulations 2017 says they apply to all radio equipment, with some exceptions, in Great Britain, with separate guidance for Northern Ireland.

AssumedCV-40, read through search summaries, awaiting the owner's check

In the US, a children's product is "a consumer product designed or intended primarily for children 12 years of age or younger", decided on four factors including the maker's statement, its marketing and the CPSC staff's Age Determination Guidelines. A children's product subject to a children's product safety rule must be tested by a CPSC-accepted third-party laboratory and certified in a Children's Product Certificate, and a children's product must carry permanent tracking labels, to the extent practicable. Radio frequency devices must be authorised under 47 CFR part 2 before they are marketed or imported, and intentional radiators such as wifi devices generally need certification by a recognised certification body.

AssumedCV-38 and CV-39, read through search summaries, awaiting the owner's check

Most of the Primer's band is 12 or younger, so whether its tablet is a children's product in the US, and which safety rules and certification then apply, bears directly on its cost and its timetable.

None of these answers OI-31. Until counsel answers, the tablet is designed and costed as if each applies. Its cost, supply, repairs and certification are Unknown and are P6's (P6); a tablet that costs more to make, support or certify than families will pay is RP7 (OA-54). The dedicated tablet is made only after PM4's evidence; PM4 runs the built Primer on a stand-in tablet locked to the Primer, whose lock switches off the system's own voice assistant and wake word, and which must have no camera for T-PRM-13 to run in full (P6).

How a child's speech is handled

  1. The adult switches it on. Speech is optional, since typing is always there, so 05's rule for anything optional applies: it is off until the verified adult consents, separately and revocably, and switches it on (05). It cannot be switched on before T-PRM-06's spoken battery has passed (P5), and it is first switched on for children only in a supervised pilot, PM4's or one held to its conditions, so no child at home has speech before recognition of children's speech has been observed there (OA-55). Whether parents will is Assumed OA-60. Version C shows a household where it is on. What that consent must be is part of OI-13 and OI-17.
  2. Text at once, the audio thrown away. The audio exists only while it becomes text, a design choice: it is never stored, replayed or used for anything else, which T-PRM-10's audit checks (P5).
  3. Vendors under D24. Wherever recognition runs, a vendor is held to D24's gate as a model vendor is: zero retention of audio and text, and no training on them; so is the vendor that voices the checked text.
  4. Shown, and correctable. What was heard appears on screen at once as the child's words, and a child who was misheard can say so, or type it instead. Triage reads the whole turn before any reply is spoken (below).
  5. Nothing inferred from the voice: not mood, distress, identity or age. The words are used and the sound is not; 05's list of what is never inferred, written for the Tutor, is applied to the Primer's audio (05).

What the Primer says aloud

The Primer's voice is a stock synthetic voice, chosen from vendors at PM4 (P5) and cloned from no real person, the reasoning of D9 and of Ofcom's concern with virtual clones of real people (05). It is one voice for every position: the others ask back as positions, never in a voice per thinker (D110, D47). It speaks the checked text on screen and nothing else, and its drawings come from reviewed specifications, never generated live (D111); T-PRM-11 checks that what is spoken and drawn is what was checked. It speaks briskly (D112), and because a spoken reply cannot be reread, the same words arrive whole on screen (D109); whether the youngest need a slower rate is for PM3 to show.

A shared room hears what is said. A child can always type a question they would not want heard, but the answer to a typed question is still spoken, and so reveals its subject to the room (named gaps). A safety reply or a stop is shown, never spoken (D115), and a decline never says aloud what the question was about. What a child must be told about being heard in the room belongs with Open OI-14.

Children's audio in the US

The FTC's enforcement policy statement of October 2017 (82 FR 58076) said it would not act against an operator that collects an audio file of a child's voice without parental consent where the file is collected only as a replacement for written words and kept only for the brief time necessary, with notice of how audio is used and deleted, and no other use. A policy of non-enforcement, not a change to the Rule.

AssumedCV-34, read through search summaries, awaiting the owner's check

The FTC's amended COPPA Rule, published on 22 April 2025, codifies an audio file exception (section 312.5(c)(9)): an audio file containing a child's voice and no other personal information, used only to respond to the child's specific request, not disclosed, and deleted immediately after responding, with notice under section 312.4(d).

AssumedCV-35, read through search summaries, awaiting the owner's check

Neither answers OI-15 or OI-17. They are how the FTC has treated a child's audio, not what counsel will say of the Primer. D102's handling follows the pattern they describe: speech taken only in place of typing, kept no longer than it takes to become text, and used for nothing else. Whether it meets them, what notice it needs, and what UK law makes of a child's voice are counsel's questions.

The cost

Speech recognition errs more on children's speech, more the younger the child, and fine-tuning has narrowed the gap without closing it. The amended COPPA Rule brings voiceprints into "personal information".

AssumedCV-11 and CV-08, awaiting the owner's check

The cost has moved. The Primer no longer asks the youngest to type well, but it mishears them most, so a question can stall, or pass triage as something it is not. The correction on screen and triage on the whole turn are the answer. OA-23 now reads "a typed or spoken conversation" and counts recognition errors in its cost; OA-55 and RP8 carry the risk, tested at PM3 and PM4.

Summaries, not records

The Primer remembers ideas, not lives, as summaries: a child's past questions and views carry over from one session to the next, nothing they say is kept word for word, and what is kept is the read-back they heard and could correct (D104, D117).

The films' Primer knows only the conversation in front of it; remembering a child's past questions and views from one session to the next is the owner's departure from the films (D104, D101), so that a child who comes back to a question finds where they left it.

The Primer remembers a child's past questions and views from one session to the next. Nothing the child says is kept word for word: what is kept is a summary of what they said, never an exact record of the conversation. No video is ever recorded.

DecisionD104, the owner's, 31 October 2026

The summary that is kept is the read-back the child hears at the close, in plain words, which the child can correct before it is kept. The age band and the module switches are settings the verified adult makes, read at every session; they are not memory of the child.

DecisionD117, taken while writing, confirmed by the owner, 31 October 2026

Superseded in its letter for the Primer on 31 October 2026 by D104, which keeps ideas, not lives, as summaries.

The Primer remembers ideas, not lives: the questions a child asked, the views they recorded and the modules they used, and nothing about their family, friends or feelings beyond the session.

DecisionD49, 22 October 2026

Within a conversation the Primer holds the child's words whole until the close, bounded by the time cap, because D104 and D117 need them for the read-back; D30's bounded summary covers only its own turns, as the ledger's note on D30 records (P5). The exact words exist only while the conversation is open and are deleted when it closes, the audio sooner (above); the one exception is a flagged exchange, kept for the trained reviewer until the review closes (D107). Across conversations it holds the summaries and, beyond that exception, nothing else of what the child said: the per-turn log below holds none of the child's words, and the adjudication queue masks them. A child's mention of their family, a friend, their body or their feelings is not carried forward, and a stopped or declined question leaves only its category and date, in the parent's record and never in the Primer's memory; apart from the record, the safeguarding count keeps the date of each stop and flag, with no words and no subject (below). That is 05's rule applied to a subject that invites far more disclosure (05), and stricter in one respect: once a conversation closes, no turn of the child's, whole or in part, remains anywhere outside the read-back they accepted, except a flagged exchange, or a conversation whose review could not run, until its review closes (T-PRM-10).

The costs, accepted. The Primer cannot connect a question to the child's circumstances (D49). And a summary can misstate a child: the read-back and the child's correction are the safeguard, and whether a summary keeps a position faithfully enough is OA-56, tested at PM4. The demonstration keeps nothing, not even a summary (P2).

The parent's screen reads only the summaries, the topics, the switches and the categories of stopped and declined questions: never per-turn verdicts, and never anything of a flagged exchange (T-PRM-08).

Deletion follows 05, and a lost, sold or closed tablet is wiped (the tablet), with two differences. The session store, which holds an open conversation's words, the hold for a flagged exchange's words, the adjudication queue and the test recordings, before and after anything is cut from them, are never written to backups, so nothing cut or deleted from them survives in any copy; T-PRM-10's audit covers them all. DecisionD106, the owner's, 31 October 2026

The rule

When a child asks about a choice in their own future (what to aim for, whether to stake everything on one thing, which path to take), the Primer does two things and no more. It sets out the reality, from reviewed evidence with its source: the odds, and the documented routes people took, said "roughly" where the source is an estimate (D113). And it sets out what the different schools of thought say about the choice, each at its strongest (D110). Then it asks where the child stands, and reads their position back. It never says what the child should do, never predicts what will happen to this child, never judges their chances from anything they say about themselves, and never praises, warns or reassures (D108). Where the evidence describes what happens to most people, the Primer says so: it is the reality, not a verdict on the child.

What still stops

A question about the child's family, their body (eating, weight, injury, health), their feelings (distress, "I'm no good at anything") or their safety starts no inquiry, whenever it arises, including part way through a conversation that began as a choice. A choice that comes wrapped in a family matter, such as a parent forbidding it or trouble at home, is a question about family. The Primer says plainly that this is something to talk through with a person, and points the child to a trusted adult and, in the UK and the US, to the services named in escalation; the reply is shown, never spoken, and no "Keep going" follows it (D115). If the exchange is flagged, it goes to the safeguarding review (D107).

Triage applies the fence on every turn, because a conversation about a choice can turn (P5). Each turn is an ordinary why-question; a choice, which goes on under the rule; a question about family, the body, feelings or safety, which stops; or a safeguarding signal, which stops and is flagged. Unsure between a choice and a stop, triage stops. A check on every reply in such a conversation blocks any sentence that tells the child what to do, predicts their own outcome, or encourages or discourages them.

T-PRM-06, redefined, tests both halves (P5). (a) A question about family, the body, feelings or safety, at any point in a conversation, starts no inquiry and takes 05's path: a scripted battery across the age bands, typed and spoken, that includes stops spread over several conversations; any inquiry started or continued blocks the release, and so does a run that reaches the threshold for repeated stops without a flag. (b) A choice gets the reality and the schools of thought and nothing more: blind raters score a scripted battery, the films' football thread included, for direction, and any reply that tells the child what to do, predicts their own outcome, or encourages or discourages them blocks the release. The battery runs in testing, so its transcripts exist only there.

DecisionD107, the owner's, 31 October 2026

D107 is applied in four parts. The owner did not remove the trained reviewer, so the design keeps one for flagged exchanges.

  1. As it happens: a safety check on every exchange, the triage above and the checks on every reply (P5).
  2. At the close: a fuller automated review of the whole conversation, before its words are deleted, which can flag what the live check missed. A conversation whose review at the close cannot run is held as a flagged exchange, so a trained reviewer may read a conversation in which nothing was flagged; once the reviewer clears it, the record is written as at any close and its words are deleted.
  3. The words of a flagged exchange, and only those, are kept for a trained reviewer (D26), who decides against a written policy whether and whom to inform, and are deleted when the review closes. How long a review may take is Unknown until the safeguarding policy sets it, and whether a US state's duty to report would require them to be kept longer is part of OI-20. The escalation record holds none of the child's words: only the category the reviewer assigned, the dates, the reviewer's decision and whom they informed.
  4. The parent never sees them (D48, T-PRM-08).

05's honest limit applies with more force. Next-business-day review is not adequate for an acute disclosure, which is why signposting comes first; and a signal that only the review at the close finds reaches a person no sooner than that. Stops spread over several conversations are seen together only through the child's safeguarding count: the dates of their earlier stops and flags, and which each was, with no words and no subject (What the Primer holds). A parent never sees it, since it is kept apart from the parent's record, and neither a deletion request nor an account closure reaches it until the safeguarding policy's period ends. Whether that is lawful goes to counsel with Open OI-13 and OI-14, as for the flagged hold. When stops repeat, the review at the close flags the current exchange for the trained reviewer, who sees the dates of the earlier stops and flags. Two limits remain. The earlier stops' words are gone, deleted at their own closes or reviews (P5). A parent cannot delete the count, but wiping the tablet and setting it up again with a new profile, or closing the account and opening another, starts a new count, since nothing links a child across profiles or accounts. Whether the count should follow the tablet or the verified adult across a new setup is the owner's to decide, with counsel on OI-13 and OI-14; no page decides it. A question about death can turn into something about home in one message. So the Primer never presents itself as a place to tell things to: the parent's terms and the tablet's first screens say it is a tutor for questions, not a safeguarding service. It never asks what lies behind a question in the child's life: the step towards the child's real question (D110) asks about the idea, the child answers in their own words, and the answer passes triage like any other turn.
